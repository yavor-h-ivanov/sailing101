Rig and sails

The mast, its wires and the sails are the engine of a sailing boat. They are also the part most likely to fail suddenly, so knowing what to look at matters more than anywhere else.

How a rig stays up

A masthead sloop is a tripod of wire. The forestay pulls forward, the backstay pulls aft, and the cap shrouds pull down each side through the spreaders. Lower shrouds stop the middle of the mast pumping. Everything is in tension; the mast is in compression and pushes down on the deck or the keel. Take any one wire away and the mast comes down.

Standing rigging

  • Stainless 1×19 wire with swaged or mechanical (Sta-Lok, Norseman) terminals. Insurers commonly want it replaced every 10–15 years regardless of appearance TBC per insurer.
  • Failure points: broken strands at the lower terminal, cracked swages, corroded chainplates hidden under deck, seized rigging screws, worn clevis pins, missing split pins.
  • Furling headsail systems wrap the forestay in a foil; the forestay inside cannot be inspected without dropping the sail and often the foil.

Running rigging and deck gear

  • Polyester braid is standard. Halyards chafe at the masthead sheave and the clutch; sheets chafe at the fairleads.
  • Winches need stripping and greasing yearly; a winch that spins freely both ways has lost its pawl springs.
  • Lines led aft to the cockpit are convenient but add friction and clutter.

Sails

  • A cruising set is a mainsail with two or three slab reefs and a furling genoa around 130–140% overlap.
  • Dacron sails last 10–20 seasons of cruising use but lose shape long before they tear. A sail that bags and hooks at the leech makes the boat heel more and point worse.
  • UV strips on furling genoas and sail covers on the main are the cheapest way to double sail life.
