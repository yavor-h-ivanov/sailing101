Seas and cruising grounds

The same boat behaves like a different boat in different waters. Tide, wind systems, harbour styles and rules vary so much that each of these seas deserves its own page in your head.

Black Sea

  • Almost tideless. Low salinity. A large enclosed sea that builds a short, steep chop quickly.
  • Summer sailing weather is generally settled; autumn and winter bring hard north-easterlies and sudden storms.
  • Coasts: Bulgaria (Varna, Balchik, Nesebar, Sozopol, Burgas), Romania (Constanța, Mangalia), Turkey's northern coast, Georgia. Marina infrastructure is thinner than in the Mediterranean.
  • Formalities: port clearance procedures and border controls between countries still matter here more than in the EU Mediterranean TBC per country.

Mediterranean

  • Negligible tide. Thermal winds that build in the afternoon and die at night. Sudden strong named winds: Mistral (Gulf of Lion), Tramontane, Meltemi (Aegean, summer northerlies), Sirocco, Libeccio.
  • Short steep seas because fetch is limited and the water is deep close inshore.
  • Stern-to mooring with lazy lines or your own anchor is the norm. Marina prices are high in season.
  • Deep water right up to the shore makes anchoring easy in bays and impossible off many beaches.

Adriatic

  • Croatia's island chains are the main cruising ground: short hops, sheltered channels, thousands of anchorages.
  • Winds: Bora (Bura), a cold, violent north-easterly that falls off the mountains with little warning, worst near Trieste, Senj and the Velebit channel; Jugo (Scirocco), a south-easterly that builds slowly with rain and swell; Maestral, the friendly afternoon north-westerly of summer.
  • Rules are enforced: vessel registration and permit, skipper's licence with VHF endorsement, crew list, and a sojourn tax TBC current rules.
  • Mooring: marinas, town quays with lazy lines, restaurant (konoba) jetties, mooring buoys in bays, national park fees.

North Sea

  • Strong tides, shallow water, sandbanks, and a lot of shipping. Wind against tide builds a nasty sea quickly.
  • Wadden Sea and the Frisian islands: drying harbours, a place where bilge keels and long keels earn their keep.
  • Offshore energy installations, traffic separation schemes and fog make electronic aids and a proper watch essential.
  • Ports of refuge are far apart on some coasts; weather windows are shorter and more important.

Baltic

  • Brackish, nearly tideless, but water levels swing with wind and pressure. Fresh water means less fouling and less corrosion.
  • Rocky skerry coasts of Sweden and Finland: precise pilotage between marked rocks, and anchoring bow-to a rock with a stern anchor.
  • Denmark and Germany: shallow sandy waters, box berths, the Kiel Canal connecting to the North Sea.
  • Short season (roughly May to September), long summer days, cold water all year. Diesel heaters are standard equipment.
  • Sweden's and Finland's right of public access allows anchoring and going ashore nearly anywhere outside private gardens and nature reserves.

English Channel

  • Some of the strongest tidal streams and largest tidal ranges in Europe, especially around the Channel Islands and the Brittany and Normandy coasts.
  • Tidal gates: Portland Bill, Start Point, the Alderney Race, Cap de la Hague. Time them right or go backwards.
  • The Dover Strait traffic separation scheme is the busiest shipping lane in the world; cross it at right angles and report where required.
  • Weather comes from the Atlantic with little warning; the Solent is sheltered, the open Channel is not.
