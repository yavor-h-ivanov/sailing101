Hull, keel and rudder

The hull keeps the water out, the keel keeps the boat upright and stops it sliding sideways, and the rudder points it where you want. On a 30-year-old GRP boat, this is also where the expensive surprises live.

GRP construction

Almost every boat in this class has a hull of glass-reinforced plastic: layers of glass cloth or mat wetted out with polyester resin in a female mould, with a pigmented gelcoat outer skin. Decks are usually a GRP sandwich with a plywood or balsa core for stiffness. A few builders went further: Sadler's 26, 29 and 34 had two hull skins with foam between them (the Sadler 32 did not; it is a conventional solid hull with a balsa-cored deck).

  • Solid GRP hull: heavy, tough, easy to repair, tolerates neglect. The norm for this class.
  • Cored deck: stiff and light, but any deck fitting that leaks lets water into the core, which rots (plywood) or goes soft (balsa).
  • Osmosis: water migrating through the gelcoat and reacting with uncured resin, producing acidic blisters. Common on 1970s–80s hulls, ugly, expensive to treat properly, and rarely dangerous.
