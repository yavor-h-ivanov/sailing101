Hull, keel and rudder

The hull keeps the water out, the keel keeps the boat upright and stops it sliding sideways, and the rudder points it where you want. On a thirty- to fifty-year-old glass-fibre boat this is also where the expensive surprises live, so this section is written for the day you stand under a boat on the hard with a torch.

Five words before anything else

GRP
Glass-reinforced plastic, or "fibreglass": layers of glass fibre soaked in liquid plastic (resin) that sets hard. Every boat on this page is built of it.
Gelcoat
The smooth, coloured outer skin of the plastic, about a millimetre thick. It is what you polish, and what osmosis lifts.
Laminate
The stack of glass-and-resin layers behind the gelcoat. That is the actual structure.
Ballast
The heavy metal, iron or lead, in or under the keel that keeps the boat upright.
Seacock
A tap on a hole in the bottom of the boat. There are usually six to ten of them. Each one can sink you.

How a GRP boat is built

A hull is made in a female mould, outside first: gelcoat is sprayed in, then layer after layer of glass is laid in by hand and wetted out with resin. Decks are usually a sandwich, two thin skins with a light core between them, because a flat panel needs thickness to be stiff. The drawing shows both, magnified.

  • Mat and cloth. Chopped-strand mat (CSM) is a felt of short fibres that soaks up resin and fills the shape; woven roving is a heavy cloth that gives strength. Builders alternate them. Early hand-laid hulls often have uneven resin, dry patches and air voids in the mat ¹.
  • Resins. Boats of the 1970s and early 1980s were built with the cheapest general-purpose polyester (orthophthalic), which absorbs water most readily. Builders moved to isophthalic resin and gelcoat during the 1980s, which resists water far better, and vinylester is used today as a barrier skin. This is the single biggest reason older boats blister and newer ones mostly do not.
  • Thickness. A hull of this size is thickest along the keel and at the bow and thinnest in the topsides; stiffness comes from bulkheads, stringers and the internal grid as much as from the skin. Figures quoted on forums vary too much to publish ².
  • Print-through is the weave of the cloth showing faintly through the gelcoat, worst on dark hulls. Cosmetic.
  • Crazing is a web of fine cracks in the brittle gelcoat only, usually at a hard spot or an old knock. A single deep crack that runs through the gelcoat into the laminate is a different thing and needs a surveyor's opinion.
  • The hull-to-deck joint is where the two mouldings meet. Most sailing boats of this class have an inward flange on the hull with the deck sitting on it, bedded in sealant and bolted or screwed; the cheaper "shoebox" joint, where the deck drops over the hull like a lid, relies on its fasteners. Leaks here are the hardest on the boat to trace, because the water travels a long way inside before it shows.

Keel types

The keel does two jobs at once: its weight rights the boat, and its area stops the boat being pushed sideways by the wind. How the designer shaped it decides how the boat sails, where it can float and what happens when it touches the bottom.

Fin keel

bolted fin, deep or shoal fin

A single blade of cast iron or lead, bolted to a stub moulded into the hull. The standard arrangement on the Bavaria 1060, the Gib’Sea 31 and the fin-keel Sadler 32, and on almost every production boat since.

How it works
  • The ballast hangs low on a short chord, so the boat is stiff for its weight and the keel behaves like a wing: it makes sideways lift efficiently when the boat moves.
  • A short keel means the boat turns in its own length under engine and steers well going astern.
Positives
  • Points highest and sails fastest of the four.
  • Most agile in a marina.
  • The keel can be inspected, re-bedded or replaced because it is a separate casting.
Negatives
  • Deepest draught, so the most places you cannot go and the hardest touch when you do.
  • Less directional stability: the helm needs more attention on a long passage.
  • Cannot dry out without legs or a cradle.
Common faults
  • The "smile": a crack opening along the front of the keel joint after a grounding (see below).
  • Rust weeping from the joint or the bolt heads.
  • Cracked or debonded floors and grid inside the hull above the keel.
What to check
  • Sight along the joint from ahead and astern: it should be a hairline, not a gap.
  • Look inside the bilge over the keel for cracked filler, rust runs and moisture around the nuts.
  • Ask when the keel was last dropped or the bolts inspected, and whether the boat has ever been aground hard.

Bilge (twin) keels

twin keels

Two shorter keels, one each side, splayed slightly outwards. Offered on the Moody 33 and the Sadler 32 because so many British boats live in tidal harbours that dry out twice a day.

How it works
  • Two keels share the ballast and area, so each is shallower. Because they are angled, the leeward keel stands more upright as the boat heels and does more of the work.
  • Standing on both keels with the rudder as a third leg, the boat sits level on the mud or sand when the tide goes out.
Positives
  • Shallower draught than the same boat with a fin.
  • Dries out upright without legs, so drying moorings and half-tide harbours are open to you.
  • Grounding is usually a gentle stop on two feet rather than a blow on one.
Negatives
  • More wetted surface, so slower in light air, and older designs point noticeably lower than a fin. Modern twin keels close the gap.
  • When heeled, waves slap under the windward keel and draught actually increases.
  • If one keel finds a hole when drying out the boat can lean over hard.
Common faults
  • Two keel joints and two sets of bolts to corrode instead of one.
  • Damage to a keel root from taking the ground unevenly.
What to check
  • Everything under Fin keel, twice.
  • Check both keels sit square to the hull from astern; a splayed or bent keel has had a hard landing.

Long keel

full keel

The keel runs along most of the underwater length and the rudder hangs on its back edge, as on the Finnsailer 35 and most motorsailers. The oldest arrangement and still the most protective.

How it works
  • Ballast is spread along a long, shallow keel, often moulded into the hull with the metal sealed inside rather than bolted on.
  • The long straight edge resists turning, so the boat holds a course by itself.
Positives
  • Tracks like a train: the best for long passages and self-steering.
  • Rudder and propeller are protected behind the keel; it takes the ground and lobster pots gracefully.
  • Shallow draught for its length.
Negatives
  • Slow to tack and poor to windward compared with a fin.
  • Hard to steer in reverse; marina manoeuvres need planning and prop walk.
  • Heavy and slow in light air.
Common faults
  • If the ballast is encapsulated, a grounding can crack the GRP skin and let water reach the metal, which then rusts and swells (see Encapsulated keels).
What to check
  • Look for cracks and repairs along the bottom and leading edge of the keel.
  • Check the rudder’s pintles and gudgeons for wear.

Centreboard and lifting keel

swing keel, dériveur lesté

A pivoting board or a lifting fin that retracts into a stub or a case in the cabin. Rare above about 25 ft because the engineering gets expensive, but the Sadler 32 and the Gib’Sea 31 "DL" were both offered this way.

How it works
  • A ballasted stub keeps the boat upright; the board adds area for going to windward and is winched or pumped up for shallow water.
Positives
  • The shallowest draught of any type: creeks, cills and drying berths that no fin can use.
  • Board up, the boat floats off a beach before a bilge-keeler.
Negatives
  • The case takes room out of the saloon.
  • A pivot pin, lifting wire, winch or hydraulic ram and seals, all under water, all needing attention.
  • Some designs do not retract fully and still cannot dry out flat.
Common faults
  • Worn pivot pin and slop in the board.
  • Corroded lifting wire.
  • Grounding loads bending the board or cracking the case.
What to check
  • Lift and lower the board with the boat out of the water and watch for play, noise or a bent board.
  • Ask when the lifting wire and pivot were last replaced.

How the keel is attached

Almost every fin and bilge keel on this class is a casting bolted to the hull. The joint is the most heavily loaded part of the boat, and the one place where a fault can be fatal. It is worth understanding exactly what is holding the keel on.

Keel bolts

keel studs

Threaded rods cast into or bolted through the keel, passing up through the stub, with nuts and large washers or backing plates inside the bilge. A 10 m boat typically has six to ten of them, and they are hidden under filler and bilge paint.

How it works
  • The bolts clamp the keel to the stub; the sealant between the two keeps water out. Sideways loads from sailing are carried by the joint faces and the floors, and the bolts hold it all together in tension.
  • With an iron keel the bolts are usually galvanised mild steel or stainless; with a lead keel, bronze or Monel. Mixing metals badly makes a battery: silicon-bronze bolts in an iron keel corrode the iron around them.
Positives
  • A bolted keel can be dropped, inspected and re-bedded by any competent yard.
  • Mild-steel bolts fail slowly and visibly, by rusting.
Negatives
  • Stainless bolts can suffer crevice corrosion in the wet, airless keel sump and look perfect until they break; rust stains around a stainless bolt are a warning, not a cosmetic issue.
  • You cannot see the bolt where it matters, inside the keel and the stub.
Common faults
  • Corroded or wasted bolts; rust runs from the nuts; weeping at the joint.
  • On the Moody 33 the studs are mild steel by design, because the builder distrusted stainless; surface rust on the nuts is common and usually superficial {ONE}. On the Sadler 32 the backing plates are plain or galvanised steel and rust, and owners replace them with stainless {ONE}.
What to check
  • Galvanised bolts more than about fifteen years old are due for inspection; the simplest check is to drop one bolt at a time, look at it, and refit it {ONE}.
  • Ultrasound from the bolt head finds a corroded or cracked bolt without dismantling anything and works with both iron and lead; X-ray works on iron keels but lead blocks it.
  • A surveyor should sight the joint, tap around the stub and inspect the floors inside.

Grounding damage and the "smile"

keel smile, matrix damage

When a fin keel hits the bottom at speed the keel is thrown backwards: its aft end drives up into the hull while the front pries down and away. The visible sign is the smile, a crack opening along the forward end of the joint; the invisible sign is cracked or debonded floors and grid inside.

How it works
  • The keel acts as a lever with the joint as its pivot. Loads that the bolts and floors were never designed for go straight into the hull structure.
  • On boats built from the mid-1980s with a bonded internal grid (a "matrix"), the grid can crack or tear away from the hull and a wooden core in the stub can lose its strength if water gets in.
Positives
  • A smile is easy to see on the hard and is the trigger for a proper inspection.
Negatives
  • The damage that matters is inside, behind furniture and under the engine, and a boat can look fine from outside.
Common faults
  • Cracks in the filler at the keel joint, especially forward.
  • Cracked bilge paint, cracked or lifted grid, movement between grid and hull, water in a cored stub.
What to check
  • After any grounding, however gentle it felt, look inside over the keel for fresh cracks; after a hard one, lift the boat and have the joint and the structure inspected. The UK Maritime and Coastguard Agency’s guidance note MGN 613 says exactly this for fin-keel GRP yachts.
  • When buying, ask directly whether the boat has been aground and look for repairs around the keel root.
Why this matters

In May 2014 the yacht Cheeki Rafiki, a 12 m Beneteau First 40.7, lost her keel in the Atlantic and four crew died. The UK Marine Accident Investigation Branch found that the boat had grounded and been repaired more than once and that the internal matrix had detached; when it inspected four sister ships that had also grounded, all four showed matrix detachment aft of the keel. Keel losses on other production yachts have followed the same pattern of hidden structural damage or bolts corroded out of sight. Every grounding is worth an hour with a torch.

Encapsulated keel

moulded-in ballast

The hull moulding continues down to form a hollow keel and the ballast, iron or lead, is lowered in and glassed over from above. There are no bolts and no joint. Used on the Contessa 32, most Nauticats and many long-keel boats; whether the Finnsailer 35’s iron ballast is encapsulated has not been confirmed {TBC}.

How it works
  • The ballast sits inside a GRP box that is part of the hull. Loads spread over the whole keel area instead of through a few bolts.
Positives
  • Nothing to corrode and nothing to drop off; often described as the strongest way to carry ballast.
  • Takes the ground and lobster-pot lines gently because the keel is part of the hull shape.
Negatives
  • A grounding that cracks the outer skin lets water reach the ballast. Iron then rusts and swells, and a wet keel is heavy and hard to dry.
  • Cannot be replaced or re-bedded; repairs are laminate repairs.
  • Usually paired with a long or fat keel, so less agile in a marina.
Common faults
  • Cracks or bulges in the skin along the bottom of the keel.
  • Rust staining bleeding through the antifouling low on the keel.
  • Damp or free water inside the keel cavity, felt as a cold, wet bilge that never dries.
What to check
  • Tap along the keel skin listening for a dull, dead sound and look for repairs at the front and bottom edges.
  • Ask whether the keel has ever been opened.

Rudders

The rudder is a foil hung under the stern on a shaft called the stock. How it is supported decides how well the boat steers, how it survives a lobster pot, and what a surveyor will want to look at.

Spade rudder

balanced rudder

A blade held only by its stock, which passes up through the hull in a tube with a bearing at each end. Found on the Bavaria 1060 and on almost everything built after 1990.

How it works
  • Part of the blade sits ahead of the stock, so water pressure helps turn it: the rudder is "balanced" and light on the helm.
  • All the load goes into the stock and the two bearings; there is nothing else holding it.
Positives
  • The most efficient rudder for sailing and manoeuvring; steers well astern.
  • Simple to build and to replace.
Negatives
  • Everything depends on the stock. A bent stock after hitting something means a new rudder, and the leading edge is unprotected against ropes and debris.
  • Bearings wear and the blade develops play.
Common faults
  • Play at the bearings; a stiff or notchy helm from swollen plastic bearings; water inside the blade.
  • Bent stock after a grounding or a collision.
What to check
  • With the boat out of the water, push the bottom of the blade sideways: about a millimetre of movement at the bearing is acceptable, more needs investigation {ONE}.
  • Turn the rudder lock to lock by hand and feel for tight spots.

Skeg-hung rudder

The blade hangs behind a fixed fin (the skeg) that is part of the hull moulding, with a bearing at the bottom as well as at the top. The Moody 33 and the Sadler 32 are built this way.

How it works
  • The skeg carries the lower bearing, so the stock is supported at both ends and the blade cannot be levered sideways.
  • The skeg also protects the leading edge and gives the boat directional stability.
Positives
  • Robust: a knock is taken by the skeg first.
  • Steadier on the helm for a long passage.
Negatives
  • Only slightly balanced, so heavier to steer and less bite going astern.
  • A partial skeg can be a weak point in itself if it is thin.
Common faults
  • Wear in the heel bearing at the bottom of the skeg.
  • Cracks where the skeg joins the hull, especially on the Moody 33, where the hull-to-skeg joint needed strengthening on some early boats.
What to check
  • Check the heel bearing for play and the skeg root for cracks or repairs.
  • Grab the blade and try to rock it: movement means bearing wear.

Keel-hung and transom-hung rudders

pintles and gudgeons

On a long-keel boat the rudder is hinged along the back edge of the keel; on smaller and older boats it hangs on the outside of the transom. Both turn on simple hinges: pins (pintles) that drop into eyes (gudgeons).

How it works
  • The hinge pins carry the blade, so there is no stock through the hull at all on a transom-hung rudder, and a keel-hung one is protected along its whole length.
Positives
  • Very strong and very protected.
  • A transom-hung rudder can be lifted off for repair without hauling the boat.
Negatives
  • Unbalanced and heavy on the helm; poor going astern.
  • A transom-hung blade is exposed in a marina and can lift out of the water when the boat heels hard.
Common faults
  • Worn pintles and gudgeons that let the blade clonk.
  • Corroded fastenings at the transom.
What to check
  • Lift the blade and feel for play at each hinge; check the fastenings inside the transom.

Rudder stock, bearings and water in the blade

rudder post

The stock is the shaft the blade turns on. The blade itself is usually GRP over a foam core, and most of them let water in.

How it works
  • The stock is stainless steel, or aluminium on many German-built boats, with a web welded to it inside the blade. It turns in plastic bearings (nylon, acetal or polyethylene) or roller bearings.
  • Water gets into the blade at the stock and along the glued seam between the two halves.
Positives
  • A rudder is a bolt-on part: it can be dropped, repaired or replaced without touching the hull.
Negatives
  • Stainless inside a wet, airless foam core is exactly where crevice corrosion happens, and you cannot see it.
  • Nylon and acetal bearings absorb water, swell and bind; roller bearings do not, but fail suddenly rather than gradually.
Common faults
  • A German magazine survey found raised moisture in about 70% of the rudders it examined {ONE}. Signs: the blade weeps for weeks after lift-out, sometimes a brown, vinegary fluid; a moisture meter reads wetter than the hull; the blade is heavy.
  • Stiff steering after launch, easing as the bearings settle.
What to check
  • Meter the blade against the hull at lift-out and look for drips from the bottom edge days later.
  • Ask whether the rudder has ever been dried, drilled or rebuilt. Rinse the lower bearing with fresh water when the boat is out.

Osmosis

Osmosis is the word every buyer of an old GRP boat learns first and understands least. It is water getting into the laminate and reacting with the resin, and it shows itself as blisters below the waterline. Ugly, often expensive, and rarely dangerous.

Osmosis

blistering, "boat pox"

Gelcoat is not waterproof, only water-resistant. Over years, water molecules pass through it into the tiny voids and dry fibres that every hand-laid hull contains, where they react with leftover chemicals in the resin to make an acidic, glycol-rich fluid. That fluid pulls in more water by osmosis, the pressure rises, and the gelcoat lifts into a dome.

How it works
  • Blisters range from pinheads in the antifouling to coins and saucers in the gelcoat; when they are pierced they weep a sour-smelling fluid.
  • Boats built before the switch to isophthalic resins in the 1980s are the usual sufferers; a well-built hull from the 1990s rarely blisters.
Positives
  • In most cases it is cosmetic: no yacht is known to have sunk from osmosis, and a blistered hull has usually been blistered for years.
  • It can be treated once and for all with an epoxy barrier.
Negatives
  • Advanced cases can degrade the laminate itself, and a heavily hydrolysed hull is genuinely weaker.
  • Treatment is expensive and slow, and a poor treatment (epoxy over a wet hull) makes things worse.
Common faults
  • Blisters, usually found at the spring lift-out.
  • High moisture readings. Surveyors use meters such as the Sovereign and the Tramex; the numbers are not comparable between meters, and what matters is the difference between the underwater hull and a dry reference such as the topsides. Most surveyors admit the meter alone cannot predict blistering that has not yet appeared {ONE}.
What to check
  • Look at the hull the day it comes out, before it dries, and again a week later.
  • A full treatment means peeling off the gelcoat, drying the hull (weeks in a shed, or one to two weeks with heated vacuum "hot-vac" panels), filling, and coating with epoxy. UK quotes for a 31–33 ft boat have run at roughly £6,000 to £7,000 over the past two decades {TWO}; ask for a written moisture record before the epoxy goes on.

  • Danger: Practical Boat Owner, will osmosis sink my boat, Craftinsure.

Through-hulls and seacocks

A cruising boat of this size has six to ten holes below the waterline: engine cooling water in, sinks and toilet in and out, the log and depth sensors, cockpit drains. Each has a fitting through the hull and, on anything modern, a valve on top of it. Knowing where every one is, and being able to shut it in the dark, is the first thing a new owner should learn.

Skin fittings and seacocks

through-hulls, sea valves

The skin fitting is a threaded tube with a flange that sits on the outside of the hull. The seacock is the valve screwed onto it inside. What they are made of decides how long they last.

How it works
  • Bronze, an alloy of copper and tin, is the traditional material and lasts for decades in sea water.
  • DZR brass ("dezincification-resistant") is a cheaper copper-zinc alloy with a little arsenic added so the zinc does not leach out; it is what most boats built since the 1990s have.
  • Ordinary brass loses its zinc in sea water, turns pink and crumbly, and can fail in under five years. Composite fittings (glass-reinforced nylon such as TruDesign or Marelon) cannot corrode at all.
Positives
  • A good bronze or composite seacock is a fit-and-forget item.
  • A ball valve is quick: a quarter turn shuts it.
Negatives
  • The European standard for metal fittings, ISO 9093-1, permits brass and asks only for a five-year corrosion life, which is why so many boats of the 1990s and 2000s were fitted with valves that are now overdue {ONE}.
  • Metal fittings need a bonding wire or an anode if stray currents are present; composite ones do not, but they are bulkier.
Common faults
  • Dezincification: pink patches, a crumbly surface, a handle that will not move.
  • Seized valves that have not been turned in years; rusted hose clips; perished hose; a fitting bedded straight onto a thin hull without a backing pad.
What to check
  • Turn every seacock at least once a season and leave the toilet and sink ones shut when you leave the boat.
  • At lift-out scrape a spot on each skin fitting: bright yellow is healthy, pink is dying. Replace brass on sight and DZR or bronze on condition; no fixed interval is published, and the UK Boat Safety Scheme examination does not cover the condition of skin fittings at all.
  • Two stainless clips on every hose, and a tapered softwood bung of the right size tied to each fitting.
Sources and confidence: seacocks

  • ISO 9093 and the five-year clause: European Parliament written question E-005075/2012. Boat Safety Scheme scope: boatsafetyscheme.org.
  • Practice: Safe Skipper, Yachting Monthly, essential seacock checks.

Antifouling and anodes

Two things go on the underwater hull every year: paint that stops weed and barnacles, and lumps of metal that corrode so that your propeller, shaft and keel bolts do not.

Antifouling paint

bottom paint

A paint that slowly releases a biocide, almost always copper since tin was banned, so that growth cannot settle. It is renewed every year or two, and the rules on what you may use now differ from sea to sea.

How it works
  • Eroding (self-polishing) paints wear away in use, exposing fresh biocide: the usual choice for a cruising boat that moves.
  • Hard paints leach biocide from a solid film that can be scrubbed and takes drying out: for fast boats and drying moorings.
  • Copper-loaded epoxy (Coppercoat is the best-known) is applied once and lasts many seasons {ONE}.
  • Biocide-free silicone or hard slippery coatings rely on growth not gripping; growing in importance as the rules tighten.
Positives
  • A clean bottom is worth half a knot and a happier engine.
  • Modern eroding paints need no sanding between coats.
Negatives
  • Copper is a poison: authorities are restricting it, some marinas forbid in-water scrubbing, and washing-down water must be captured in many yards.
  • The wrong paint on an aluminium saildrive leg corrodes it; use a copper-free product there.
Common faults
  • Heavy fouling from a paint that was not authorised for your water, or that was applied over a slippery old coat.
  • Flaking where too many seasons have built up.
What to check
  • Check the rules for the water you keep the boat in before buying paint (table below).
  • Scrape a small patch at lift-out to see how many layers are on the hull; a thick, cracked build-up should be taken back to the gelcoat.

Sacrificial anodes

zincs

Lumps of a metal more willing to corrode than your shaft, propeller, keel bolts or saildrive leg, bolted on so that they dissolve instead. Which metal depends on the water.

How it works
  • Two different metals in sea water connected by a wire make a battery, and the less noble one corrodes. An anode is deliberately the least noble metal on the boat.
  • It only protects what it is electrically connected to: bolted directly to the shaft or leg, or joined by a bonding wire inside the boat.
Positives
  • Cheap insurance for a propeller and shaft, and essential for an aluminium saildrive leg.
Negatives
  • The wrong metal either does nothing or vanishes in weeks.
  • Anodes that are painted over, or fitted to metal that is not bonded, protect nothing.
Common faults
  • An anode that is still shiny after a season is not working; one that is gone after three months means a stray-current or bonding problem.
  • Magnesium anodes used in sea water are consumed in days.
What to check
  • Sea water: zinc, or aluminium alloy, which lasts longer for the same size. Brackish water such as the Baltic: aluminium; one popular guide recommends magnesium, but the engine makers’ guidance is that magnesium is for fresh water only {TWO}. Fresh water: magnesium.
  • Fit anodes big enough that they are no more than about half consumed at the end of the season, and never mix anode metals on one bonding system.

The deck: core and joints

Cored deck and core rot

soft deck, balsa rot

Decks and coachroofs on this class are almost all sandwiches: a thin outer skin, a core of end-grain balsa or plywood, a thin inner skin. Stiff and light, until water gets into the core. The Sadler 32 and the Moody 33 both have balsa-cored decks, the Moody with plywood pads under the fittings; the Bavaria 1060 has a sandwich deck of unstated core {ONE}; the Gib’Sea 31 and Finnsailer 35 are {TBC}.

How it works
  • The two skins carry the loads and the core keeps them apart, like the flanges and web of a beam.
  • Every bolt through the deck passes through the core. When the sealant under the fitting fails, water follows the bolt into the core, and balsa soaks it up like a sponge; plywood wicks along the grain and delaminates.
Positives
  • A sound cored deck is stiffer and lighter than a solid one and does not sweat inside.
Negatives
  • Wet core is invisible from outside for years.
  • Repair means cutting a skin off, replacing the core and re-laminating: cheap materials, expensive labour.
Common faults
  • Soft, springy spots when you walk, usually around stanchion bases, chainplates, the mast step, windlass and cleats.
  • Staining and drips on the cabin ceiling; weeping around bolt heads inside lockers; fittings that keep coming loose.
What to check
  • Walk the whole deck in bare feet and press hard around every fitting.
  • Tap with a small plastic hammer: crisp is sound, dull is delaminated. A surveyor will add a moisture meter and sometimes an infrared camera; none of the three is foolproof on its own.
  • When re-bedding a fitting, drill the holes oversize, fill them with epoxy and re-drill, so the core is sealed from the bolt for good.

The hull in one afternoon: a buyer’s checklist

  1. Walk round twice. First for the shape: sight along the topsides for flats and bumps from repairs, and along the keel joint from ahead and astern. Then for the surface: blisters, crazing, repairs, rust runs.
  2. Keel. Joint hairline or smile? Rust at the joint? Inside, over the keel: cracked filler, wet nuts, cracked or lifted grid.
  3. Rudder. Rock it for play, turn it for stiffness, look for drips from the bottom edge and cracks at the skeg root.
  4. Every hole. Count the skin fittings outside, then find each seacock inside. Scrape, turn, look at the hose and the clips.
  5. Deck. Bare feet and a plastic hammer around every fitting; ceiling and lockers for stains.
  6. Ask three questions. Has she been aground? When were the keel bolts last looked at? When was she last metered for moisture, and what did it read against the topsides?
  7. Then pay a surveyor. Out of the water, chosen by you. This list is for deciding which boats are worth the survey fee.

Terms used in this section

  • Gelcoat The coloured resin skin on the outside of a GRP moulding, about a millimetre thick, sprayed into the mould first. It gives the shine and most of the water resistance.
  • Laminate The layers of glass fibre and resin behind the gelcoat that form the actual structure.
  • Chopped-strand mat (CSM) A felt of short glass fibres that soaks up resin and follows curves. Bulk and shape rather than strength.
  • Woven roving A heavy woven glass cloth laid between layers of mat for strength.
  • Resin The liquid plastic that soaks the glass and sets hard. Polyester on all boats of this class; the cheap orthophthalic type until the 1980s, the more water-resistant isophthalic type afterwards; vinylester and epoxy are better still and dearer.
  • Core The light filling, usually end-grain balsa or plywood, between the two skins of a sandwich deck.
  • Outer and inner skin The two thin GRP layers on either side of a deck core. Together with the core they act like a beam.
  • Inner skin The GRP layer on the cabin side of a cored deck, usually thinner than the outer one.
  • Inside surface The rough, unfinished inner face of a hull laminate, seen inside lockers.
  • Deck fitting Anything bolted through the deck: stanchion bases, cleats, tracks, the windlass. Each bolt hole is a path for water into the core unless it is sealed.
  • Bedding (sealant bed) The flexible sealant under a deck fitting that keeps water out. It fails after ten to twenty years and must be renewed.
  • Core rot Wet, soft or rotten core inside a deck sandwich, felt as a springy deck.
  • Backing plate A metal or plywood plate inside the boat that spreads the load of a bolted fitting over the laminate.
  • Print-through The weave of the cloth showing faintly through the gelcoat. Cosmetic.
  • Crazing A web of fine cracks in the gelcoat only, at a hard spot or an old impact. Cosmetic, unlike a deep crack into the laminate.
  • Hull-to-deck joint Where the deck moulding meets the hull moulding, usually on an inward flange, bedded and bolted. The hardest leak on the boat to find.
  • Fin keel A single short, deep keel bolted to the hull.
  • Bilge keels Two shallower keels, one each side, so the boat can dry out standing on them.
  • Long keel A keel that runs along most of the hull with the rudder hung on its back edge.
  • Centreboard A board that swings or lifts up into a case or a ballasted stub to reduce draught.
  • Keel stub The shallow moulded extension of the hull that a bolted keel is fastened to.
  • Keel casting The cast-iron or lead keel itself. Iron is cheaper and rusts; lead is denser, softer and dearer.
  • Keel bolts The threaded rods that hold a bolted keel to the stub, with nuts inside the bilge.
  • Keel joint The sealed face between keel and stub. A hairline when healthy.
  • The smile A crack opening at the forward end of the keel joint after a grounding.
  • Floors and grid The internal ribs across the bottom of the hull that spread keel loads; on boats built since the mid-1980s often a bonded-in moulded grid, the matrix.
  • Encapsulated keel Ballast sealed inside the hull moulding, with no bolts.
  • Spade rudder A rudder blade held only by its stock.
  • Skeg-hung rudder A rudder supported at the bottom by a fixed fin, the skeg.
  • Keel-hung rudder A rudder hinged on the trailing edge of a long keel.
  • Transom-hung rudder A rudder hung on the outside of the stern.
  • Rudder stock The shaft the rudder blade turns on, stainless steel or aluminium.
  • Rudder bearings The bushes the stock turns in: plastic or roller. Play here is felt as a clonk at the wheel.
  • Quadrant The fan-shaped fitting on top of the stock that the steering cables pull on; on a tiller boat, the tiller head does the job.
  • Pintles and gudgeons The pins and eyes that hinge a keel-hung or transom-hung rudder.
  • Gudgeon The eye half of a rudder hinge; the pintle is the pin.
  • Tiller A lever fixed straight onto the rudder stock, the simplest steering there is.
  • Osmosis Water passing through the gelcoat into the laminate and reacting with the resin to form blisters.
  • Blister A dome in the gelcoat raised by fluid underneath. Pierce one and it weeps a sour liquid.
  • Void A tiny air pocket or dry patch in the laminate where water can collect.
  • Permeation Water molecules passing slowly through a coating that is water-resistant but not waterproof.
  • Hydrolysis The chemical reaction between water and polyester resin that makes the acidic fluid inside a blister.
  • Moisture meter An electronic meter held against the hull that indicates water in the laminate. Readings differ between makes; the comparison with a dry area of the same hull is what counts.
  • Gelcoat peeling Machining the gelcoat off a blistered hull so it can dry before an epoxy barrier coat is applied.
  • Epoxy barrier coat Several coats of epoxy over a dried, peeled hull to keep water out for good.
  • Skin fitting A threaded tube with a flange that passes through the hull; the outside half of a through-hull.
  • Backing pad A plywood or GRP pad inside the hull that gives a skin fitting a flat, strong seat.
  • Seacock The valve on top of a skin fitting. Handle in line with the pipe means open; across it means shut.
  • Hose tail The ribbed spigot the hose pushes onto.
  • Hose clips Stainless-steel bands that clamp the hose to the tail. Two on every underwater hose.
  • Reinforced hose Wire- or fabric-reinforced hose that will not kink or collapse and is rated for below-waterline use.
  • Softwood bung A tapered wooden plug that can be hammered into a broken fitting to stop a leak. One tied to each seacock.
  • DZR brass Dezincification-resistant brass, a copper-zinc alloy with arsenic added so the zinc does not leach out in sea water. Better than plain brass, not as good as bronze.
  • Dezincification Sea water dissolving the zinc out of brass, leaving a pink, porous, weak copper sponge.
  • Antifouling Paint on the underwater hull that releases a biocide, usually copper, to stop weed and barnacles growing.
  • Anode A block of zinc, aluminium or magnesium bolted to the boat to corrode instead of the metal parts it is connected to.
  • Galvanic corrosion What happens when two different metals sit in sea water and are electrically connected: the less noble one dissolves.
  • Bonding Wiring the underwater metal parts together inside the boat so that one anode protects them all.
  • Hot-vac Heated vacuum panels applied to a peeled hull to dry it in a week or two instead of months.
  • Survey A professional inspection of the boat out of the water, with a written report. Always get one before buying a boat of this age.
