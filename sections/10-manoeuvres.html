Manoeuvres under engine

Nobody is watching when you sail beautifully offshore. Everybody is watching when you berth. A five-tonne boat with one propeller, no brakes and a keel that wants to pivot behaves nothing like a car, and berthing it is learnable: slowly, with a plan, with the crew briefed, and with the wind and tide on your side. In a hurry? Jump to coming alongside, stern-to or the crew briefing.

Seven words before anything else
Prop walk
The sideways kick of the stern when the propeller turns, strongest in astern.
Pivot point
The point the boat turns about, about a third of the way back from the bow when going ahead.
Steerage way
Enough speed through the water for the rudder to work. Below it, the boat does not steer.
Warps and springs
Warps are mooring lines. Springs are the two that run diagonally and stop the boat moving forward or back.
Fenders
Air-filled cushions hung over the side between the boat and the pontoon or another boat.
Windage
How much the wind pushes the boat: the bow, with little keel under it, blows off fastest.
Slip a line
Let go a line that is doubled back on board, so it can be pulled in without anyone ashore.

The physics you must accept

  • Prop walk. In astern the propeller pushes the stern sideways before the boat starts to move. A right-handed propeller (clockwise seen from astern when going ahead) walks the stern to port. It is strong on shaft drives and long keels, weaker on saildrives.
  • The pivot point is about a third of the way aft from the bow going ahead, and moves aft going astern. Turning ahead, the stern swings out: leave room for it beside pontoons and other boats.
  • No water flow, no steering. At very low speed the rudder does nothing. A short burst of ahead (a “kick”) over the rudder turns the stern without building speed.
  • Wind on the bow blows it off faster than you can correct at low speed. Wind and tide decide your approach, not your preference: approach into whichever is stronger.
  • Keel type matters. Fin-keel and saildrive boats (Bavaria 1060, Gib’Sea 33) steer well astern once moving; a long-keel boat (Finnsailer 35) barely steers astern at all and turns wide.

Finding your boat’s prop walk

right-handed, left-handed, paddle wheel effect

Ten minutes in open water with no wind tells you more than any book. Do it before the first time you berth the boat.

How it works
  • With the boat stopped, put the engine astern at moderate revs and watch the stern: the way it kicks is the way it will always kick. Then try going ahead from stopped with the rudder central, and turning a full circle each way ahead and astern.
Positives
  • Once known, prop walk is a tool: berth on the side the stern walks towards, and a burst astern both stops the boat and pulls the stern in.
Negatives
  • On the wrong side, a burst astern throws the stern away from the pontoon just as you step off.
Common faults
  • A boat whose owner does not know which way it walks; a propeller changed for one of the other hand after a new engine, so the old habits are wrong.
What to check
  • Write the answer on a card by the engine controls: “walks to port in astern”.

Lines, fenders and knots

  • Lines: at least four, each longer than the boat, plus two longer ones for springs and awkward berths; about 12 to 14 mm braided polyester on a boat of this size TBC. Chafe where they pass through fairleads kills them.
  • Fenders: four to six, sized for the boat TBC, and a fender board for rough walls. Hang them at the height of the pontoon, not the waterline, and adjust them before arriving.
  • Knots: the bowline (a loop that will not slip or jam: the loop of a mooring line), the round turn and two half hitches (to a ring or post, and can be undone under load), the clove hitch (quick, for fenders on a guardrail), and the cleat hitch (a turn, figures of eight and a locking turn on a cleat). Learn them until your hands tie them in the dark.

Coming alongside

  1. Plan on the way in: which side, which lines, where the wind and tide come from, where to stop. Fenders and lines ready on that side, lines led outside everything and back on board.
  2. Brief the crew: who steps off with which line, and in what order (see the briefing).
  3. Approach into the wind or tide, whichever is stronger, at a shallow angle and at the slowest speed that still steers.
  4. Neutral early; steer to lie parallel as the bow reaches the pontoon; a short burst astern to stop.
  5. Step off, never jump, with the midships line or the bow line, and take a turn round a cleat at once; then the stern line, then the springs.
  6. Never put a hand or foot between the boat and the pontoon to fend off. Let the fenders do it.

Leaving a berth: springing off

  1. Take off every line except the one spring you need; double it back (round the pontoon cleat and back to the boat) so it can be slipped.
  2. A fender at the point that will press on the pontoon: the bow for a bow spring, the quarter for a stern spring.
  3. Stern out: gently ahead against the bow spring, steering as if to turn towards the pontoon (on a tiller, push it away from the pontoon): the stern swings out. When it points well clear, neutral, slip the spring, and reverse out.
  4. Bow out: gently astern against the stern spring: the bow swings out. When clear, neutral, slip the spring, and motor ahead.
  5. Get the slipped line aboard fast, before it finds the propeller.

Stern-to with lazy lines

  1. Fenders out on both sides, stern lines ready at the stern (one each side), the passerelle or plank ready, and someone with a boathook.
  2. Line up the gap well out, then reverse in slowly and straight; prop walk will try to swing the stern, so start the approach allowing for it and use short bursts ahead to correct.
  3. A crew member steps ashore or passes the stern lines to the marina staff; take a turn on board at once.
  4. Pick up the lazy line at the stern, walk it forward outside the guardrails and everything else, and pull the heavy mooring line up to a bow cleat until the boat is held off the quay. Keep the lazy line out of the propeller: neutral while it is in the water.
  5. Adjust the stern lines so the stern is a step or a plank’s length from the quay.

Where there are no lazy lines, the boat lays its own anchor: drop it three or four boat lengths out TBC, reverse in paying out chain, take the stern lines ashore, then tighten the chain. Bows-to is the same with the anchor off the stern, and gives more privacy from the quay.

Box berths between posts

  1. Prepare two long stern lines with a big loop in each, and two bow lines; fenders are rarely needed, except at the bow.
  2. Choose a box the right width: boxes are marked with the maximum beam or length on the posts in some harbours TBC.
  3. Enter slowly, bow first; as the stern passes the posts, drop the loop over the windward post first, then the leeward one; the helm keeps a little way on.
  4. Stop with a burst astern while the crew steps ashore from the bow with the bow lines; then tighten the stern lines so the boat is held off the quay.

Mooring buoys, anchoring and rafting

  • Picking up a buoy: approach slowly into the wind or tide, whichever is stronger, so the boat stops at the buoy; the crew on the bow points at it, because the helm loses sight of it under the bow. Pick it up with a boathook and pass your own line through the buoy’s ring or strop, never tie to the small pick-up buoy alone.
  • Anchoring: choose a spot with room to swing, the right depth and a good seabed on the chart; approach into the wind; stop; lower the anchor to the bottom and pay out chain as the boat drifts back (how much is in Deck hardware); set it with a gentle burst astern; then check it holds, with two lined-up landmarks (a transit) or the plotter’s anchor alarm.
  • Swinging room: at anchor the boat swings round the anchor in a circle as big as the chain let out; so do the boats near you, which may have different amounts of chain.
  • Rafting up: alongside another boat, with fenders between, your own bow and stern lines to the shore or pontoon as well as lines to the other boat, and springs; the biggest boat on the inside. Cross the other boat by its foredeck, never its cockpit.

The crew briefing: what to say so nobody jumps

  1. “We are going alongside port side to, bow into the wind.”
  2. “Fenders on the port side at pontoon height; lines at the bow, the stern and midships, led outside the guardrails and back on board.”
  3. “(Name), you step off with the midships line when I say, and take a turn on the nearest cleat. Step, do not jump.”
  4. “(Name), you take the bow line; I will take the stern.”
  5. “If it goes wrong, we go round again. Nobody fends off with hands or feet.”

Say it before you enter the harbour, not on the approach, and ask each person to say back what they are doing.

Terms used in this section

  • Going astern Moving backwards under engine.
  • Stern kick The sideways push of the stern from prop walk, strongest as the propeller starts turning in astern.
  • Propeller hand A right-handed propeller turns clockwise, seen from astern, going ahead; a left-handed one the other way. The hand sets the direction of prop walk.
  • Pivot point The point the boat turns about: about a third of its length from the bow going ahead, further aft going astern.
  • Stern swing When the boat turns ahead, the stern swings out much further than the bow swings in.
  • Bow line A mooring line from the bow forward to the pontoon or quay.
  • Stern line A mooring line from the stern aft to the pontoon or quay.
  • Spring from the bow A line from the bow leading aft to the pontoon; it stops the boat moving forward. Used to spring the stern out.
  • Spring from the stern A line from the stern leading forward to the pontoon; it stops the boat moving back. Used to spring the bow out.
  • Fenders Air-filled cushions hung between hull and pontoon at pontoon height.
  • Bow spring, doubled back The bow spring led round the pontoon cleat and back on board, so it can be let go from the boat.
  • Bow fender A fender at the bow, where the boat pivots against the pontoon when springing the stern out.
  • Stern out The stern swinging away from the pontoon as the boat motors gently ahead against the bow spring.
  • Stern spring, doubled back The stern spring led round the pontoon cleat and back on board, ready to slip.
  • Quarter fender A fender at the quarter, where the boat pivots when springing the bow out.
  • Bow out The bow swinging away from the pontoon as the boat motors gently astern against the stern spring.
  • Wind or tide from ahead Approach into whichever is stronger: it slows the boat and makes it steerable at low speed.
  • Approach track A shallow angle to the pontoon, slowly.
  • Neutral early Take the engine out of gear well before the pontoon and let the boat’s way carry it in.
  • Burst astern A short push in astern to stop the boat; with the right propeller hand it also pulls the stern in.
  • Stepping off The crew step, never jump, onto the pontoon, with a line, and take a turn on a cleat at once.
  • Fenders on both sides Stern-to, the boats either side are touching distance away.
  • Stern lines Two lines from the stern quarters to bollards or rings on the quay, often crossed.
  • Lazy line A light line tied at the quay that leads to the heavy mooring line; picked up at the stern and walked forward.
  • Mooring line The heavy line from the ground chain, made fast at the bow to hold the boat off the quay.
  • Ground chain A heavy chain along the harbour bed that the mooring lines are fixed to.
  • Box posts Wooden or steel posts at the outer end of a box berth.
  • Stern lines to the posts Lines with big loops dropped over the posts as the boat passes, windward first.
  • Bow lines to the quay Lines from the bow to the quay, taken ashore by a crew member stepping off the bow.
  • Crosswind Wind across the box: take the windward lines first, and keep a little way on.
  • Bowline A fixed loop that does not slip or jam, and can be undone after a load.
  • Cleat hitch A turn round the cleat, figures of eight over its horns, and a locking turn.
  • Transit Two fixed objects seen in line; if they stop lining up while at anchor, the anchor is dragging.
