Licences and qualifications

Whether you legally need a licence depends on the flag of the boat, the waters you are in, and sometimes the size of the engine or the boat. Whether you should get trained does not depend on anything: a week’s course teaches more than a season of trial and error, and makes the trial and error safer. Jump to the ICC, radio licences or the boat’s papers.

Which rules apply to you

Many countries do not require a licence to sail a private yacht of this size under their own flag, but expect a visiting foreign skipper to hold whatever their own country would require, or a recognised certificate such as the ICC. Others require a licence of everyone in their waters. And almost everywhere, anyone using a marine VHF radio needs an operator’s certificate, and the boat needs a radio licence.

Training: the RYA ladder

The Royal Yachting Association’s scheme is the best known in Europe, and its certificates are recognised in many countries and by most charter companies ²; other countries have their own (see below). Courses are run by recognised training centres, on their boats or yours.

  • Competent Crew (five days afloat TBC): steering, sail handling, ropework and safety; being useful on board. No experience needed; a shorter taster course exists too.
  • Day Skipper: the theory course (in a classroom or online) covers chartwork, tides, the collision rules, weather and safety; the practical course (about five days aboard TBC) covers skippering a yacht on short passages by day in waters you know.
  • Coastal Skipper: longer passages, night sailing and more demanding conditions, with its own theory course, shared with the Yachtmaster.
  • Yachtmaster Coastal and Offshore (and, above them, Yachtmaster Ocean, for ocean passages and astronavigation): exams, not courses, with minimum sea time and miles logged; the Offshore certificate with a commercial endorsement is a professional qualification TBC.
  • Supporting courses: the VHF Short Range Certificate (SRC), First Aid, Sea Survival, Diesel Engine and Radar. The SRC and the diesel course are the two most useful to a new owner.

The International Certificate of Competence (ICC)

The ICC comes from UNECE Resolution 40, a resolution of the United Nations Economic Commission for Europe that each country chooses whether to apply. It lets a country issue a certificate that other countries can accept as evidence that a skipper is competent. It is the document most Mediterranean, Adriatic and inland-waterway authorities, and many charter companies, want to see from a visiting skipper TBC.

  • Getting one: in the UK the RYA issues it to eligible applicants who hold the Day Skipper practical certificate (or higher), or who pass an ICC assessment at a training centre TBC. Other countries that apply the resolution issue their own, often against their national licence.
  • Categories: it states what it covers: sail or power, coastal or inland waters, and the size of boat TBC. A CEVNI endorsement, after a short test on the European inland waterway rules, is needed for the rivers and canals of mainland Europe.
  • Limits: not every country accepts it. A country usually issues its ICC only to its own citizens or residents, with some exceptions, so an EU resident cannot simply get one from the RYA; some countries accept an ICC only if its holder is a citizen or resident of the country that issued it; and some refuse an ICC issued abroad to their own nationals TBC. It is renewed after a period, often five years TBC.

Radio: two licences

  • The operator’s certificate for the person: the Short Range Certificate (SRC), a one-day course and exam, covers VHF and DSC. Anyone may use the radio in a real distress emergency; otherwise the operator, or the person supervising, needs one TBC.
  • The ship radio licence for the boat, from the flag state (in the UK, from Ofcom). It comes with the boat’s call sign and its MMSI, the nine-digit number programmed into the DSC radio and the AIS (see Electronics). When a boat changes hands the licence and MMSI are usually re-issued to the new owner: do not keep using the old owner’s TBC. When a boat changes flag it gets a new MMSI, because the first three digits are the country’s code: the DSC radio and the AIS must be reprogrammed (some only by a dealer), and the EPIRB recoded with the new country and re-registered. A PLB is registered to its owner, not the boat.
  • A handheld DSC VHF has its own MMSI and, in the UK, its own licence (a ship portable radio licence) TBC (see Electronics).

The boat’s own papers

Carry the originals, or certified copies where the law allows, in a waterproof folder by the chart table.

  • Registration. Most countries require a yacht going abroad to be registered: in the UK on Part 1 of the register (full title) or the cheaper Small Ships Register, which records the boat but not its ownership TBC.
  • Proof of ownership: the bill of sale, and the builder’s certificate where it survives.
  • VAT status: inside the EU, evidence that VAT has been paid on the boat, or that it is exempt. A boat without it can be charged VAT at the full rate when it is found TBC. The rules for boats entering the EU from outside, including from the UK, are in Buying abroad.
  • Insurance: third-party cover is compulsory in many countries, often with a minimum amount, and marinas ask to see the certificate TBC. Check that the policy covers the waters you are going to.
  • Radio licence and the skipper’s certificates.
  • A crew list, with names, nationalities and passport numbers, is asked for in many countries, and required for entering from outside the Schengen area TBC.
  • Local papers: Croatia’s vignette and tourist tax receipt, Turkey’s transit log, and so on.

Crossing a border by sea

customs, immigration, Schengen, Brexit

Between two countries of the Schengen area, a yacht with EU, EEA or Swiss crew usually sails from one to the other with no passport control TBC; between two countries of the EU customs union, with no customs. The two are not the same: Norway is in Schengen but outside the EU customs union, and Ireland and Cyprus are in the EU but outside Schengen TBC. Local permits, taxes and reporting apply either way. Everywhere else, arriving by sea is like arriving by air: the boat and each person on it must enter the country properly.

What happens
  • Arriving from outside the area, the skipper goes first to a port of entry, flies the yellow Q flag if the country still asks for it, and reports to customs and immigration (in person, by phone, or online) before anyone else goes ashore TBC.
The good news
  • Inside Schengen there is normally no passport control, and inside the EU customs union no customs, for a VAT-paid boat and EU, EEA or Swiss crew; local rules still apply, such as Croatia’s vignette, Greece’s cruising tax (TEPAI) and reporting to the harbour master, and temporary border checks are possible TBC.
The bad news
  • Since the UK left the EU, a crossing between the UK and France, Belgium or the Netherlands is a customs and immigration crossing in both directions. Non-EU crew, British crew included, can stay in the Schengen area for 90 days in any 180, and are now registered in the EU’s Entry/Exit System when they arrive; non-British crew entering the UK may need an Electronic Travel Authorisation, and the UK asks for an online pleasure craft report TBC.
Signs
  • A crew member who stays aboard without being cleared in; a boat whose VAT status cannot be shown; a boat that forgot to clear out of one country before entering the next.
What to do
  • Read the entry rules for each country before you go, carry the papers above, and ask the marina or harbour master on arrival: they deal with it every day.

Choosing a sailing school

  • Look for a centre recognised by the national body (the RYA, or its equivalent), with instructors qualified for the course.
  • Ask the maximum number of students per boat (usually five, with the instructor TBC) and how many hours a day are spent sailing.
  • Do the practical course on a boat like the one you will sail, or on your own boat: many schools offer own-boat tuition.
  • Do the theory course first, in the winter. The practical week goes further if the chartwork and the collision rules are already familiar.

Terms used in this section

Which rules apply

  • Flag state The country a boat is registered in. Its laws apply to the boat wherever it goes.
  • Coastal state The country whose waters the boat is in. Its rules apply to every boat there, whatever its flag.
  • Charter requirements What a charter company and its insurer ask of a skipper and crew; often more than the law.

Training

  • Competent Crew The RYA’s first practical course: being useful and safe on board.
  • Day Skipper The RYA course for skippering a yacht by day in familiar waters; the usual first target for an owner.
  • Day Skipper theory The shore-based course in chartwork, tides, collision rules, weather and safety.
  • Coastal Skipper The RYA course for longer passages, including at night.
  • Coastal Skipper / Yachtmaster theory The advanced shore-based course, shared by the Coastal Skipper and the Yachtmaster exams.
  • Yachtmaster Coastal An exam, with minimum sea time, showing competence to skipper on coastal passages.
  • Yachtmaster Offshore An exam, with more sea time and passages, showing competence to skipper offshore.
  • ICC The International Certificate of Competence, under UNECE Resolution 40: a certificate other countries can accept as proof of competence.
  • Supporting courses Short courses alongside the ladder: the VHF Short Range Certificate, First Aid, Sea Survival, Diesel Engine and Radar.
