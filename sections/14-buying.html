Buying and owning

A 30-year-old boat can be a bargain or a hole in the water. The difference is usually visible before you buy, if you know where to look, and the running costs are predictable if you are honest about them.

Before you look at boats

  • Decide what the boat is for: weekends, a summer cruise, living aboard, crossing seas. That decides keel, cockpit, rig, heating and budget.
  • Sail on a few. Charter, crew for someone, or do a course on a similar boat.
  • Budget the purchase price plus 10–20% for the first year's catching up TBC rule of thumb, plus the annual running costs below.

Viewing checklist (short version)

  • Hull: blisters, repairs, keel joint "smile", rudder play, through-hull condition.
  • Deck: soft spots, crazing, leaking windows and fittings, staining below.
  • Rig: age of standing rigging, broken strands, chainplates, sail condition.
  • Engine: hours, service history, starts from cold, exhaust colour, oil condition, mounts, gland.
  • Systems: batteries date, wiring standard, gas installation, heads and holding tank.
  • Papers: title, registration, VAT, engine and rigging invoices, survey history.

The survey

Always, for a boat of this age, out of the water, by a surveyor you chose and who is not connected to the broker. The survey is also your negotiating document and your first year's job list.

Running costs

  • Berthing (the biggest cost, hugely variable by sea and country)
  • Insurance
  • Annual lift-out, antifouling, anodes
  • Engine service
  • Replacements on a cycle: standing rigging, sails, batteries, running rigging, upholstery, electronics
  • The unplanned item, every year
