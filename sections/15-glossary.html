#

1×19 wire
Stainless-steel rigging wire of nineteen strands twisted into one: stiff, low-stretch, 5 to 7 mm on this class. Dyform is the same idea with compacted strands; rod is a solid bar. In: Rig and sails.

A

Abeam
At right angles to the centreline, out to the side. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Absorption and float
Two stages of battery charging: absorption holds a set voltage until the battery is nearly full; float then holds a lower voltage to keep it full. In: Boat systems.
Accumulator
A small tank with a diaphragm and an air cushion that stores pressure so the pump does not cycle constantly. In: Boat systems.
Aft cabin
The stern cabin. On a centre-cockpit boat it is a separate room with its own hatch; on aft-cockpit boats from around 1980 onwards, and on most built after 1985, it is tucked under one side of the cockpit. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Ahead / astern
In front of / behind the boat. Also the two gear directions of the engine. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Air intake and filter
Where the engine breathes. A choked air filter gives black smoke and lost power. In: Engine and drivetrain.
AIS target
A ship or boat shown by AIS, with its name, course, speed and a line showing where it will be in a few minutes. In: Electronics.
Alternator
The engine’s generator, driven by the belt; it charges the batteries while the engine runs. In: Engine and drivetrain, Boat systems.
Amidships
The middle of the boat, lengthwise. "Midships!" as a steering order means centre the wheel. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Amp-hour (Ah)
A unit of battery capacity or use: one amp for one hour. In: Boat systems.
Anchor
The hook. It holds by digging into the seabed, not by weight, and a modern design holds several times better than the one the boat was born with. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Anchor chain
Calibrated galvanised chain, 8 or 10 mm on this class, made to a standard link size so it fits the gypsy. Its weight is the shock absorber. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Anchor locker
The bow locker where the anchor chain is stowed. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Anode
A block of zinc, aluminium or magnesium bolted to the boat to corrode instead of the metal parts it is connected to. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Anti-siphon valve
A valve at the top of a loop in the raw-water hose that lets air in when the engine stops, so the sea cannot siphon into the exhaust and the engine. Needed when the injection point is near or below the waterline. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Antifouling
Paint below the waterline that slows weed and barnacle growth. Renewed every year or two. In: Anatomy and terminology, Hull, keel and rudder.
Apparent wind
The wind felt on a moving boat: the true wind combined with the wind of the boat’s own motion. In: Electronics, Sailing fundamentals.
At anchor
One all-round white light (two for a vessel of 50 m or more), one black ball by day. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Athwartships / fore-and-aft
Across the boat / along the boat. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Autopilot drive
The motor or ram that moves the rudder for the autopilot: a tiller pilot on a tiller, a belt-driven wheel pilot, or a ram on the quadrant below deck. In: Deck hardware and steering.

B

Babystay
A short inner forestay from part way up the mast to the foredeck that stops the middle of the mast pumping. The 2002 Gib’Sea 33 has one. In: Rig and sails.
Backed jib
A jib held on the windward side, as when hove to; the wind pushes it the wrong way and turns the bow off. In: Sailing fundamentals.
Backing pad
A plywood or GRP pad inside the hull that gives a skin fitting a flat, strong seat. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Backing plate
A metal or plywood plate inside the boat that spreads the load of a bolted fitting over the laminate. In: Hull, keel and rudder, Deck hardware and steering.
Backstay
Wire from the masthead to the stern. Stops the mast falling forwards. A split backstay forks to both quarters; an adjuster lets you tension it. In: Anatomy and terminology, Rig and sails.
Ball valve
A valve with a drilled ball inside: a quarter turn of the handle lines the hole up with the pipe (open) or across it (shut). In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Ballast
The weight, usually cast iron or lead, built into or bolted under the keel to keep the boat upright. Roughly 30–45% of the boat's weight on this class. In: Anatomy and terminology, Sailing fundamentals.
Bathing platform / boarding ladder
Stern access to the water. Usual on boats built since the 1990s, rare on the older British boats, which make do with a ladder hooked over the side. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Battens
Flat stiffeners in pockets along the back edge of the mainsail. In: Anatomy and terminology, Rig and sails.
Battery charger
A mains charger that runs on shore power. Set it for the battery type. In: Boat systems.
Battery management system (BMS)
The electronics inside a lithium battery that protect it, by disconnecting it, from over-charge, deep discharge and charging in frost. In: Boat systems.
Battery monitor
A display that counts amp-hours in and out through the shunt and shows the state of charge. In: Boat systems.
Beam reach
The wind at 90° to the boat, on the beam; the sails about halfway out. Often the fastest point of sail. In: Sailing fundamentals.
Bear away
Turn the bow away from the wind. In: Sailing fundamentals.
Beating
Sailing upwind in a series of tacks, zigzagging across the wind. In: Sailing fundamentals.
Bedding (sealant bed)
The flexible sealant under a deck fitting or skin fitting that keeps water out. It ages and must be renewed; owners talk of ten to twenty years, but there is no fixed life. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Bevel gearbox
The right-angle gearbox at each end of a rod steering system, at the pedestal and at the rudder stock. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Bilge
The lowest inside part of the hull, under the floor, where water collects. The bilge pump empties it; know where its handle is kept. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Bilge keels
Two shallower keels, one each side, so the boat can dry out standing on them. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Bilge pump outlet
The discharge through the hull, above the waterline, reached through a high loop so the sea cannot flow back. In: Boat systems.
Bilge pump supply
The automatic bilge pump’s own fused supply, straight from the battery, so it works with the house switch off. In: Boat systems.
Bimini
A sun awning over the cockpit. Common on Mediterranean boats. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Binnacle
The housing of the steering compass, usually on top of the wheel pedestal. In: Electronics.
Black and grey water
Black water is toilet waste; grey water is from sinks and showers. Some countries ban discharging both. In: Boat systems.
Blister
A dome in the gelcoat raised by fluid underneath. Pierce one and it weeps a sour liquid. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Blown out
A sail that has stretched into a deep bag with its draft aft and a hooked leech: more heel, more weather helm, less speed. In: Rig and sails.
Bonded window
A thick acrylic pane glued to the outside of the cabin with a flexible adhesive and no frame. Fails when the pane crazes or the adhesive lets go. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Bonding
Wiring the underwater metal parts together inside the boat so that one anode protects them all. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Boom
The horizontal spar along the bottom of the mainsail. It swings across the boat when you tack or gybe (turn so the wind crosses to the other side). It hurts. In: Anatomy and terminology, Rig and sails.
Boot top
The painted stripe just above the waterline that hides the dirty mark the water leaves. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Bosun’s chair
The seat you are winched up the mast in. Always with a second, safety halyard tied to your harness. In: Rig and sails.
Bow
The front end. Forward is towards it. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Bow line
A mooring line from the bow forward to the pontoon or quay: it holds the bow in and stops the boat drifting back. In: Manoeuvres under engine.
Bow roller
The fitting at the stem over which the anchor chain runs, and where the anchor usually lives. In: Anatomy and terminology, Deck hardware and steering.
Bow spring, doubled back
The bow spring led round the pontoon cleat and back on board, so it can be let go from the boat. In: Manoeuvres under engine.
Bowline
A fixed loop that does not slip or jam, and can be undone after a load. In: Manoeuvres under engine.
Box posts
Wooden or steel posts at the outer end of a box berth. In: Manoeuvres under engine.
Bridgedeck
The raised step between the cockpit and the companionway that stops a cockpit full of water pouring below. The mainsheet traveller often sits on it. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Broach
An uncontrolled turn up into the wind, usually running in a strong wind with too much sail up. In: Sailing fundamentals.
Broad reach
The wind behind the beam, the sails well out; fast and comfortable. In: Sailing fundamentals.
Bubble tester
A small window of liquid in the gas pipe: with everything off, press the button, and bubbles mean a leak. In: Boat systems.
Burst astern
A short push in astern to stop the boat; port side to with a right-handed propeller, it also pulls the stern in. In: Manoeuvres under engine.
Butane
The gas in blue Calor cylinders; stops vaporising near 0 °C, so it fails in the cold. In: Boat systems.
Butyl tape
A sticky, non-hardening sealant tape for bedding fittings: stays flexible for decades, comes apart again, no cure time, but is not a glue. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Bypass valve
A valve that joins the two sides of a hydraulic ram. Open, the wheels are disconnected so the emergency tiller can move the rudder; closed, the wheels and any hydraulic autopilot steer. Open by mistake, there is no steering. In: Deck hardware and steering.

C

Call sign
The boat’s radio name in letters and numbers, issued with the ship radio licence. In: Electronics.
Calorifier
An insulated hot-water tank heated by engine coolant through a coil, and by an electric element on shore power. In: Boat systems.
Calorifier coil
The coil inside the calorifier that engine coolant flows through; it heats the water without mixing with it. In: Boat systems.
Cam cleat
Two spring-loaded toothed jaws that grip a rope pulled down between them and release with a flick upward; for sheets and control lines. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Cap cloud
A bank of cloud sitting on the mountain crest as the bora pours over it: the classic warning sign. In: Seas and cruising grounds.
Cap shrouds
The shrouds that run from the masthead over the spreader tips down to the chainplates. In: Anatomy and terminology, Rig and sails.
Carbon monoxide alarm
An alarm to BS EN 50291-2 that detects the odourless gas from any burning fuel. One where people sleep. In: Boat systems.
Catenary
The curve a chain hangs in between bow and anchor. Its weight keeps the pull on the anchor horizontal until the wind straightens it out. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Centre cockpit / aft cockpit
Where the cockpit sits. A centre cockpit (Moody 33) leaves room for a separate aft cabin behind it; an aft cockpit (Sadler 32, Bavaria 1060) is more common and gives a larger saloon. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Centre of effort
The point where the sails’ total push is centred. In: Sailing fundamentals.
Centre of lateral resistance
The point where the hull and keel’s resistance to being pushed sideways is centred. In: Sailing fundamentals.
Centreboard
A board that swings or lifts up into a case or a ballasted stub to reduce draught. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Centreline
The imaginary line from bow to stern down the middle of the boat. The mast and the keel sit on it. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Chafe
Wear from a rope or sail rubbing on something, over and over. The commonest way ropes and sails die. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Chain locker
The compartment right in the bow where the anchor chain falls. In: Anatomy and terminology, Deck hardware and steering.
Chain stopper
A hinged catch (a pawl) on the foredeck that drops onto a link and holds the chain, so the windlass is not left carrying the load. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Chainplate
The stainless strap the shroud attaches to, passing through the deck and bolted to a bulkhead or knee. The place a Moody or a Sadler leaks. In: Rig and sails.
Chainplate leak
Water past the cover plate on deck into the balsa core and the plywood bulkhead below, hidden until the deck goes soft or the strap corrodes in the slot. In: Rig and sails.
Chainplates
The metal straps bolted to the hull or deck that the shrouds (the wires holding the mast up sideways) attach to, usually three a side for the cap shroud and the two lowers. A classic leak and corrosion point. In: Anatomy and terminology, Buying and owning.
Channel 16
The international VHF distress, safety and calling channel. Keep a watch on it at sea. In: Electronics.
Channel plan
The list of which VHF channel does what. Europe uses the international plan; sets bought in America may be set to theirs. In: Electronics.
Chart datum
The level charted depths are measured from; in tidal waters about the lowest astronomical tide (LAT), so the sea rarely falls below it. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Chart table (navigation station)
Where the charts (sea maps), instruments and electrical switch panel live, usually beside the companionway. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Charted depth
The depth below chart datum printed on the chart. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Chartplotter
A screen at the helm showing the chart and your position; many also show AIS, radar and instruments. In: Electronics.
Chopped-strand mat (CSM)
A felt of short glass fibres that soaks up resin and follows curves. Bulk and shape rather than strength. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Claw anchor
The Bruce and its copies: sets easily, holds modestly, sits well on a bow roller. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Cleat
A fitting for tying a rope to. There are usually two at the bow, two amidships (near the mast, for the springs, the diagonal mooring ropes) and two at the stern. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Cleat backing plate
A plate under the deck that spreads a cleat’s load across the laminate; without one the bolts pull through, which is where most cleat failures happen. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Cleat hitch
A turn round the cleat, figures of eight over its horns, and a locking turn. In: Manoeuvres under engine.
Clevis pin
The stout cross-pin that joins a fork to an eye, held in by a split pin. In: Rig and sails.
Clew
The back lower corner, where the outhaul (main) or the sheets (genoa) attach. In: Rig and sails.
Close-hauled
Sailing as close to the wind as the boat will go, about 45° off it, with the sails pulled in tight. Also called beating. In: Sailing fundamentals.
Close reach
Between close-hauled and a beam reach, the sails a little eased. In: Sailing fundamentals.
Clutch (jammer)
A lever that grips a rope so you can take it off the winch and use the winch for another rope. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Coachroof
The raised cabin top, with the coachroof windows in its sides. Also called the cabin top. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Coaming
The raised edge around the cockpit that keeps water out and gives you a backrest. The primary winches usually sit on it. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Coastal Skipper
The RYA course for longer passages, including at night. In: Licences and qualifications.
Coastal Skipper / Yachtmaster theory
The advanced shore-based course, shared by the Coastal Skipper and the Yachtmaster exams. In: Licences and qualifications.
Coastal state
The country whose waters the boat is in. Its rules apply to every boat there, whatever its flag. In: Licences and qualifications.
Coaxial cable
The round antenna cable with a central wire inside a braided screen. Water in it ruins VHF range. In: Electronics.
Cockpit
The sunken, open area near the back where the crew sits and steers. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Cockpit drains
Pipes from the cockpit floor to the sea, so a wave that fills the cockpit empties itself. In: Anatomy and terminology, Deck hardware and steering.
Cockpit locker
Deep stowage under the cockpit seats for fenders, mooring ropes and everything else. In: Anatomy and terminology.
COLREGs
The International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea: the rules of the road for every vessel. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Companionway
The opening and steps from the cockpit down into the cabin, closed by a sliding hatch on top and washboards in front. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Companionway steps
The ladder down from the cockpit. On most aft-cockpit boats the engine sits right behind it. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Compass north
Where the boat’s own steering compass points, pulled a little by the boat’s iron and wiring. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Competent Crew
The RYA’s first practical course: being useful and safe on board. In: Licences and qualifications.
Compression post
The pillar or bulkhead under a deck-stepped mast that carries its downward load to the keel. In: Rig and sails.
Compression test
A gauge screwed into each cylinder in turn to measure how well it seals; low or uneven readings mean wear. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Constrained by her draught
A deep ship that cannot leave the deep water: three all-round red lights, a cylinder. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Core
The light filling, usually end-grain balsa or plywood, between the two skins of a sandwich deck. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Core rot
Wet, soft or rotten core inside a deck sandwich, felt as a springy deck. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Course and speed over the ground
The GPS’s track and speed relative to the seabed, which include the effect of the tide or current. In: Electronics.
CPA and TCPA
Closest point of approach, and time to it: how near an AIS target will pass, and when, if neither boat changes course or speed. In: Electronics.
Crazing
A web of fine cracks in the gelcoat only, at a hard spot or an old impact. Cosmetic, unlike a deep crack into the laminate. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Crazing (acrylic)
A web of fine cracks in an acrylic pane or hatch lid from sun, stress and harsh cleaners. Hatch lids craze first because they face the sky. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Crevice corrosion
Corrosion of stainless steel that starts in tight, wet gaps starved of oxygen: under a washer, inside a rudder, in a keel sump. Invisible until it fails. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Crevice corrosion (rigging)
Stainless steel rusting from inside a tight, wet, airless gap: the top of a swage, the slot round a chainplate, under a cover plate. A rust stain is the warning. In: Rig and sails.
Cruising chute
An asymmetric spinnaker cut for short-handed sailing, set from the bow without a pole and handled through a snuffer (a sock). In: Rig and sails.
Cunningham
Tensions the front edge of the mainsail from just above the tack. In: Anatomy and terminology, Rig and sails.
Cutless bearing and P-bracket
A rubber-lined, water-lubricated bearing near the propeller, carried in a bronze strut (the P-bracket). Play here means a new one. In: Engine and drivetrain.

D

Dacron and laminates
Dacron is woven polyester, the cheap, tough, shape-losing cloth on almost all cruising sails; laminates are lighter and hold shape but die sooner. In: Rig and sails.
Danbuoy
A floating pole with a flag, thrown to a person overboard so that they can be seen from further away. In: Sailing fundamentals.
Day Skipper
The RYA course for skippering a yacht by day in familiar waters; the usual first target for an owner. In: Licences and qualifications.
Day Skipper theory
The shore-based course in chartwork, tides, collision rules, weather and safety. In: Licences and qualifications.
Dead reckoning
A position worked out from course and distance run since the last known position. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Deck fitting
Anything bolted through the deck: stanchion bases, cleats, tracks, the windlass. Each bolt hole is a path for water into the core unless it is sealed. In: Hull, keel and rudder, Deck hardware and steering.
Deck pump-out
The deck fitting a marina’s pump-out hose connects to. In: Boat systems.
Depth of water
Charted depth plus the height of tide: what the sounder should read (allowing for where its transducer is). In: Navigation and passage planning.
Depth transducer
The sensor in the hull that sends and hears the sound pulses of the depth sounder. In: Electronics.
Deviation
The error of a compass caused by iron and electrical fields on the boat, different on each heading. In: Electronics, Navigation and passage planning.
Dezincification
Sea water dissolving the zinc out of brass, leaving a pink, porous, weak copper sponge. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Diesel bug
Bacteria, moulds and yeasts that grow where water meets diesel in a tank, forming a slime that blocks filters. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Diesel heater
A sealed burner that draws diesel from the tank and blows warm air through ducts; Eberspächer and Webasto are the usual makes. In: Boat systems.
Dipstick
A steel rod reaching into the sump; pulled out and wiped, it shows the oil level between two marks. Check it cold and level, every day you use the engine. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Direction of buoyage
The direction lateral marks are arranged for: from the sea into harbour, or along a coast as shown on the chart. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Dished deck
A sunken area around the mast step: the core under the plate has got wet and crushed, or the post below has given. In: Rig and sails.
Displacement
The boat's weight, equal to the water it pushes aside. About 3.7 to 6.2 tonnes for the five boats on this page. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Distribution panel
The panel of breakers or fuses, one per circuit, fed through the house switch. In: Boat systems.
Dorade vent
A hooded air scoop on deck with a box under it, so air gets in but water drains back out. Named after the 1930s yacht that first carried them. In: Anatomy and terminology, Boat systems.
Draught (draft)
Depth from the waterline to the bottom of the keel. Decides where you can float: 1.1 m for a Finnsailer 35, up to 1.7 m for a deep-fin Sadler 32. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Drive belt
The rubber belt from the crankshaft pulley to the alternator and, usually, the coolant pump. Loose, it squeals and charges poorly; worn, it breaks. Carry a spare. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Drying height
The height of a rock or bank above chart datum, underlined on the chart; it is uncovered at low tide. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Drying out
Sitting on the seabed as the tide goes out. Bilge keels stand up by themselves; a fin keel needs legs, a cradle or a wall to lean on. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
DSC
Digital selective calling: sends a digital alert or call, with the boat’s MMSI and position, on channel 70 TBC. In: Electronics.
Dyneema
A very strong, very low-stretch polyethylene fibre used in halyard cores. Slippery and melts at about 150 °C. In: Rig and sails.
DZR brass
Dezincification-resistant brass, a copper-zinc alloy with arsenic added so the zinc does not leach out in sea water. Better than plain brass, not as good as bronze. In: Hull, keel and rudder.

E

East cardinal
Black, yellow, black, two cones base to base, three quick flashes. Safe water is to its east. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Electric bilge pump
A submersible pump in the lowest point of the bilge. Rated at zero lift, so it pumps less than the box says. In: Boat systems.
Emergency link switch
A switch that joins the two banks, to start the engine from the house bank if the start battery is flat. Normally off. In: Boat systems.
Emergency tiller
A bar that fits a square socket on the stock head, under a deck plate, when the wheel system fails. Fit it in harbour before you need it at sea. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Emergency wreck marking buoy
Blue and yellow vertical stripes, a yellow upright cross, alternating blue and yellow flashes: a new wreck not yet on the charts. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Encapsulated keel
Ballast sealed inside the hull moulding, with no bolts. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Engine bay
Under the cockpit floor or behind the companionway steps on an aft-cockpit boat; under the cockpit on a centre-cockpit boat; under the wheelhouse floor on a motorsailer. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Engine block
The main casting of the engine, holding the cylinders and crankshaft. On an indirectly cooled engine the coolant flows through passages inside it. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Engine fuel filter
The fine filter on the engine before the injection pump. Bleed point 1. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Engine mounts
Rubber blocks between the engine’s feet and its bed that stop vibration reaching the hull. They sag, crack and soften in diesel. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Engine seacock
The valve on the cooling-water intake in the bottom of the boat. Open before starting, closed when leaving the boat. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Engine switch
The battery switch for the start battery, alternator and starter. Never off while the engine runs. In: Boat systems.
EPIRB
Emergency position-indicating radio beacon: the boat’s satellite distress beacon, on 406 MHz TBC. Register it. In: Electronics.
Epoxy barrier coat
Several coats of epoxy over a dried, peeled hull to keep water out. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Epoxy ring
The hole through a cored deck drilled oversize, filled with epoxy and redrilled, so the bolt passes through solid plastic and a leak cannot reach the core. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Exhaust (mixing) elbow
The bend where sea water is sprayed into the hot exhaust. It corrodes and chokes with carbon from the inside; a routine replacement on older engines. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Exhaust outlet
Where the exhaust and cooling water leave the boat, usually in the transom. Look for water here every time you start. In: Engine and drivetrain.

F

Fairlead
A fitting that guides a rope over the deck edge without rubbing it through, but does not hold it. In: Anatomy and terminology, Deck hardware and steering.
Fall wind
Air falling down a mountain slope and speeding up; the bora is one, the mistral partly one. In: Seas and cruising grounds.
FAME (biodiesel)
Fatty acid methyl ester, blended into road diesel: up to 7 % under the European EN 590 standard (B7). It attracts water and feeds diesel bug. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Fault current
The large current that flows when a live wire touches earth; it is what trips a breaker or RCD. In: Boat systems.
Feathering propeller
Blades that turn edge-on to the water when sailing and reverse their pitch for astern. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Fender
The inflated rubber bumper you hang over the side to keep the hull off the pontoon or the boat next door. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Fenders
Air-filled cushions hung between hull and pontoon at pontoon height. In: Manoeuvres under engine.
Fetch
The distance of open water the wind has blown across. The longer the fetch, the bigger the waves. In: Seas and cruising grounds.
Fin keel
A single short, deep keel bolted to the hull. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Fishing other than trawling
With nets or lines out: red over white all-round lights, two cones point to point. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Fix
A position: a GPS fix is the receiver’s calculated position. “No fix” means it cannot calculate one. In: Electronics.
Fixed propeller
Blades fixed in position: cheap, strong and the most drag under sail. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Flag state
The country a boat is registered in. Its laws apply to the boat wherever it goes. In: Licences and qualifications.
Flange
A lip or rim: on the hull, the inward ledge the deck sits on; on a skin fitting, the rim that sits against the outside of the hull. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Flat fluke anchor
Danforth and the aluminium Fortress: two flat flukes on a hinged stock. Superb in sand and mud, poor in weed and rock, folds flat; the usual kedge. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Float switch
A switch that starts the electric pump automatically when the water rises. In: Boat systems.
Floors and grid
The internal ribs across the bottom of the hull that spread keel loads; on boats built since the mid-1980s often a bonded-in moulded grid, the matrix. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Fluxgate compass
An electronic compass sensor that gives the heading to the autopilot and instruments. Mounted low and central, away from iron. In: Electronics.
Folding propeller
Blades hinged so that they fold shut behind the hub when sailing and swing open when the shaft turns. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Forecabin
The bow cabin, usually with a V-shaped double berth. Lively at sea, lovely in harbour. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Foredeck
The deck forward of the mast, where the anchor and the headsail are handled. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Forehatch
The hatch on the foredeck over the forecabin. It doubles as the emergency exit. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Forepeak
The very front compartment, in front of the forecabin's forward wall, where the anchor chain lives. Sailors also use the word loosely for the forecabin. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Forestay
Wire from the masthead to the bow. Stops the mast falling backwards and carries the headsail, usually inside a furler. In: Anatomy and terminology, Rig and sails.
Forestay sag
The forestay bowing to leeward under the pull of the headsail. More backstay tension means less sag and a flatter, higher-pointing genoa. In: Rig and sails.
Framed window
A pane clamped between aluminium frames with a rubber gasket, the frame bedded to the cabin side. Leaks at the gasket and at the frame bolts as both age. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Freeboard
Height of the deck above the water. About a metre on this class. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Fuel filler
The deck cap you fill through. Its seal keeps rain and spray out of the tank; do not confuse it with the water filler. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Fuel shut-off valve
A valve at or near the tank that cuts the fuel off, for filter changes and in a fire. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Fuel tank
Steel, aluminium or plastic, under a cockpit locker or berth. Water and sludge collect in its bottom. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Furler
A drum at the bottom of the forestay and a hollow tube (the foil) around the wire: pull the furling line and the genoa rolls up around the tube like a blind. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Furler foil
The grooved aluminium tube over the forestay that the genoa’s luff slides up and that rotates to roll the sail. In: Rig and sails.
Furling drum
The spool at the bottom of the foil that the furling line winds on and off. In: Rig and sails.
Furling jam
A furling mainsail stuck half in and half out because the roll was loose or creased; the reason some sailors refuse in-mast furling. In: Rig and sails.
Furling line
Rolls the headsail up on its furler. In: Anatomy and terminology.

G

Galley
The kitchen. The cooker swings on gimbals so it stays level as the boat heels. Almost always beside the companionway so the cook gets air. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Galvanic corrosion
What happens when two different metals sit in sea water and are electrically connected: the less noble one dissolves. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Galvanic isolator
A device in the shore earth wire that blocks the small currents between boats that eat anodes, while still passing a fault current. In: Boat systems.
Gas alarm
A sensor low in the bilge near the galley that sounds and closes the solenoid valve when it detects gas. In: Boat systems.
Gas cylinder
Butane or propane, stood upright and strapped in the locker. Different in every country. In: Boat systems.
Gas locker
A sealed locker for the cooking-gas bottles with a drain overboard, so leaking gas cannot collect in the bottom of the boat. Often built into the lazarette area, but never simply "in" it. In: Anatomy and terminology, Deck hardware and steering, Boat systems.
Gas locker drain
The pipe from the bottom of the gas locker that carries leaking gas overboard. Test it with a bucket of water. In: Boat systems.
Gear cable
The push-pull cable from the cockpit lever to the gearbox. A stretched or loose cable gives a gearbox that will not fully engage. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Gearbox
Bolted behind the engine: ahead, neutral and astern, usually with a reduction. Worked by a cable from the cockpit lever. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Gelcoat
The coloured resin skin on the outside of a GRP moulding, a fraction of a millimetre thick, sprayed into the mould first. It gives the shine and most of the water resistance. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Gelcoat peeling
Machining the gelcoat off a blistered hull so it can dry before an epoxy barrier coat is applied. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Genoa
A large headsail whose back corner reaches aft of the mast. Sized by overlap: a "135% genoa" reaches back a distance equal to 135% of the mast-to-forestay distance. In: Anatomy and terminology, Rig and sails.
Genoa car
The sliding block (a pulley on a slider) on the side-deck track that sets the sheet angle. Forward for a fuller sail, aft for a flatter one. In: Rig and sails.
Genoa car and track
The sliding block on the side-deck track that sets the genoa sheet angle; pin-stop cars move only when the sheet is slack, ball-bearing cars move under load. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Genoa sheets
The two ropes, one each side, from the genoa’s clew back through a car on the track to the primary winches. In: Rig and sails.
Genoa track and car
A track along each side deck with a sliding fitting (the car) that holds the pulley the genoa sheet runs through, so you can change the angle of pull. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Gimballed cooker
A cooker hung on pivots so it stays level as the boat heels and rolls; each burner should have a flame-failure device. In: Boat systems.
Gland drip
The slow drip of a packed gland while the shaft turns, which lubricates and cools the packing. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Gland hose and clips
The short, reinforced rubber hose that joins the stern gland to the stern tube, with two stainless clips at each end. It is below the waterline. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Gooseneck
The hinge that joins the boom to the mast and lets it swing sideways and lift. In: Anatomy and terminology, Rig and sails.
GPS and AIS antennas
Small antennas, often on the pushpit, for the position receiver and, on some boats, a separate AIS antenna. In: Electronics.
Grab bag
A waterproof bag of essentials (handheld VHF, beacon, flares, water) ready to take into the liferaft. In: Electronics.
Grab rails
The wooden or steel handrails along the coachroof and below decks. "One hand for the boat" means one hand on one of these. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Ground chain
A heavy chain along the harbour bed that the mooring lines are fixed to. In: Manoeuvres under engine.
Ground track
The line over the seabed from where you are to where you are going; drawn with two arrowheads. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Grounding
Touching the bottom. Gentle groundings are part of sailing; a hard one at speed is a structural event for a fin keel. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
GT (gross tonnage)
A measure of a ship’s internal volume, used to decide which rules apply. International sewage rules start at 400 GT, far above any yacht. In: Boat systems.
Guardrails and stanchions
The two wires along each side of the deck (also called lifelines) and the vertical posts that hold them up. About 60 cm high; the lower wire is there to stop a foot going through. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Guardrails (lifelines)
The two wires along each side from pulpit to pushpit; upper one at least 600 mm above the deck by the racing rules. Plastic-covered wire hides its own rust. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Gudgeon
The eye half of a rudder hinge; the pintle is the pin. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Guy
The windward spinnaker sheet, which runs through the end of the pole and sets the pole's angle. In: Anatomy and terminology.
“Gybe-oh”
The helm’s call as the stern passes through the wind and the boom crosses. In: Sailing fundamentals.

H

Halyard
A line that hoists a sail up the mast: main halyard, genoa halyard, spinnaker halyard. In: Anatomy and terminology, Rig and sails.
Halyard swivel
The bearing at the top of the foil that lets the sail and foil turn while the halyard stays still. In: Rig and sails.
Halyard winches
Smaller winches on the coachroof (or on the mast on older boats) for hoisting sails and for reefing (making them smaller). In: Anatomy and terminology.
Halyard wrap
The commonest furler fault: a halyard leaving the swivel at too flat an angle winds round the foil, jams the furler and can eventually break the forestay. In: Rig and sails.
Hanging locker
The wardrobe. Often opposite the heads. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Hard spot
A place where a bulkhead or fitting stops the hull flexing, so the gelcoat cracks there first. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Hatch
Any opening in the deck with a lid. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Head, tack, clew
The three corners of a sail: top, front-bottom, back-bottom. ("Tack" the corner is a different word from "tack" the turn, and from "on port tack", which says which side the wind is on.) In: Anatomy and terminology.
Head to wind
The moment in a tack when the bow points straight into the wind and the sails flap. In: Sailing fundamentals.
Head up
Turn the bow towards the wind (also: luff up). In: Sailing fundamentals.
Header tank and coolant cap
The coolant filler, usually on top of the heat exchanger, with a pressure cap. Check the level cold; never open it hot. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Heads
The toilet compartment, and the toilet itself. The word comes from the days when the crew's toilet was at the head (the bow) of the ship. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Heads pump
The hand pump beside the bowl that flushes and empties it. In: Boat systems.
Headsail
Any sail forward of the mast. A genoa is large and overlaps the mast; a jib is smaller and does not; a storm jib is tiny and heavy. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Heat exchanger
A tube bundle where the sea water, in the tubes, takes heat from the coolant around them. It blocks with scale and impeller debris. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Heave to
Stop the boat under sail by backing the jib and lashing the helm to leeward. In: Sailing fundamentals.
Heel
The boat leaning over under the push of the wind. Normal; heavy heel is slow. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Heel bearing
The bearing at the very bottom of a skeg-hung or keel-hung rudder, where the blade’s heel sits on the skeg or keel. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Heel corrosion
Aluminium at the mast heel rotting where rain that ran down inside the mast pools against stainless fastenings and bilge water. Only visible with the mast out. In: Rig and sails.
Height of a light
Measured from the chart’s high-water level to the light itself; the higher, the further it can be seen. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Height of tide
How far the sea is above chart datum at a given time, from the tide tables. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Helm
The steering position, the wheel or tiller itself, and the person steering ("who has the helm?"). In: Anatomy and terminology.
Helm pump
The small hand pump behind a hydraulic steering wheel; turning the wheel pumps oil to the ram. In: Deck hardware and steering.
High loop (gooseneck)
A loop in the exhaust hose high under the deck, which stops following seas driving water back up the exhaust. In: Engine and drivetrain.
High-pressure pipes
Steel pipes from the injection pump to the injectors. Never loosen them with the engine running: the fuel is at a pressure that can pierce skin. Cracked open at the injector, only if the engine still will not start, they are bleed point 3. In: Engine and drivetrain.
High-water alarm
A second float, higher than the first, that sounds a siren if the water keeps rising. In: Boat systems.
High-water level
The level heights of lights and clearances are measured from: mean high water springs (MHWS) or highest astronomical tide (HAT), as the chart states. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Holding tank
Stores toilet waste for pumping out ashore. Required in many of the seas covered here. In: Anatomy and terminology, Boat systems.
Holding-tank vent
A vent pipe from the holding tank to the hull side, with a carbon filter against the smell. In: Boat systems.
Horn cleat
Two horns on a stem, for a figure-of-eight of rope. Length rule of thumb: 25 mm for every 1.5 mm of rope diameter. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Hose clips
Stainless-steel bands that clamp the hose to the tail. Two on every underwater hose. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Hose tail
The ribbed spigot the hose pushes onto. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Hot-vac
Heated vacuum panels applied to a peeled hull to dry it in a week or two instead of months. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Houdini hatch
A toughened-glass hatch in an aluminium frame, common on 1970s and 1980s British boats; its glass seal cannot be replaced, so a leaking one is replaced whole. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Hounds
The point on a fractional rig where the forestay meets the mast, below the top. A 7/8 rig has its hounds seven-eighths of the way up. In: Rig and sails.
House bank
The battery or batteries that run everything except the starter: lights, instruments, fridge, autopilot. In: Boat systems.
House switch
The battery switch that connects the house bank to the panel. Off when leaving the boat. In: Boat systems.
How sails attach
The mainsail's front edge runs up a groove in the mast on slides or a rope edge; a furling genoa's front edge runs up the groove in the furler tube; an old-style jib clips to the forestay with hanks. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Hull-to-deck joint
Where the deck moulding meets the hull moulding, usually on an inward flange, bedded and bolted. The hardest leak on the boat to find. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Hydraulic hoses
The two hoses that carry oil between the helm pump and the ram. Air or a leak in them means a helm that turns without steering. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Hydraulic ram
The cylinder whose piston pushes a tiller arm on the stock. Its end seals are where hydraulic systems leak. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Hydrolock
Water in a cylinder: it does not compress, so the engine stops with a thud or breaks. Usually caused by cranking too long, or by siphoning. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Hydrolysis
The chemical reaction between water and polyester resin that makes the acidic fluid inside a blister. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Hypoid oil
A thick gear oil (grades such as 80W-90) used in gearboxes and the Sadler’s oil-fed stern gland. In: Engine and drivetrain.

I

ICC
The International Certificate of Competence, under UNECE Resolution 40: a certificate other countries can accept as proof of competence. In: Licences and qualifications.
Immersion heater
An electric element in the calorifier, worked by shore power. In: Boat systems.
In irons
Stuck head to wind, sails flapping, with no way on and no steering. In: Sailing fundamentals.
In-mast furling
A mainsail that rolls up inside a hollow, slotted mast. Convenient; no roach; jams if it is furled badly or the sail is old. In: Rig and sails.
Inboard / outboard
Towards the centreline / towards or beyond the side of the boat. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Injection pump
Raises the fuel to very high pressure and sends it to each injector in turn. The throttle and stop levers are on it. Bleed point 2; specialist work beyond that. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Injectors
Nozzles in the cylinder head that spray fuel into each cylinder as a fine mist, at very high pressure. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Inlet seacock
The valve on the toilet’s sea-water intake. Closed after use and whenever the boat is left. In: Boat systems.
Isolated danger mark
Black with red bands, two black balls, white light Fl(2): a small danger right under it, with safe water all round. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Isolating valve
A hand valve by the cooker that shuts off its supply. In: Boat systems.
Isolation transformer
A transformer that passes shore power to the boat magnetically, with no wire connection to the shore earth: the safest and dearest way to stop galvanic corrosion. In: Boat systems.

J

Jackstays
Flat webbing straps running along each side deck from cockpit to bow. In rough weather you clip your safety harness to them before leaving the cockpit. In: Anatomy and terminology, Deck hardware and steering.
Joker valve
A rubber duckbill valve at the toilet outlet that stops waste flowing back. Change it every year. In: Boat systems.

K

Kedge
A second, lighter anchor, often a flat fluke type, for holding the stern, a second line in a gale or pulling the boat off when it has run aground. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Keel
The fixed fin under the hull. Its heavy weight (the ballast) pulls the boat upright again after it heels, and its area stops the boat sliding sideways. Fin, bilge (twin) or long keel: see Hull, keel and rudder. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Keel bolts
The threaded rods that hold a bolted keel to the stub, with nuts inside the bilge. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Keel casting
The cast-iron or lead keel itself. Iron is cheaper and rusts; lead is denser, softer and dearer. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Keel-hung rudder
A rudder hinged on the trailing (back) edge of a long keel, with its stock in a tube and its heel in a bearing on the keel. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Keel joint
The sealed face between keel and stub. A hairline when healthy. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Keel stub
The shallow moulded extension of the hull that a bolted keel is fastened to. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Kicker (vang)
A strut or rope-and-pulley system (a tackle) from the foot of the mast to the boom that stops the boom lifting. A rod kicker also holds the boom up when the sail is down. In: Anatomy and terminology, Rig and sails.
Knot
One nautical mile an hour. Also a thing you tie. In: Navigation and passage planning.

L

Laminate
The layers of glass fibre and resin behind the gelcoat that form the actual structure. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Land breeze
A lighter night-time offshore wind, as the land cools below the sea’s temperature. In: Seas and cruising grounds.
Lazarette
The stern locker, right aft, often holding the steering gear and the emergency tiller. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Lazy line
A light line tied at the quay that leads to the heavy mooring line; picked up at the stern and walked forward. In: Manoeuvres under engine.
Lazyjacks and stackpack
Lines from the mast to the boom that catch the mainsail as it comes down, often with a zipped sail cover (the stackpack) fixed to the boom. In: Anatomy and terminology, Rig and sails.
Leak-down test
A gas test with a pressure gauge: pressurise the pipe, close the cylinder, and see whether the pressure falls. In: Boat systems.
“Lee-oh”
The helm’s call as the tack begins (the tiller goes to leeward). In: Sailing fundamentals.
Lee shore
A shore the wind is blowing onto. Dangerous: a boat in trouble drifts towards it. In: Sailing fundamentals.
Leech
The back, free edge of a sail. Where a tired sail hooks and a good one streams. In: Rig and sails.
Leech line
A thin cord sewn into the leech that is tightened just enough to stop the edge fluttering. In: Rig and sails.
Leech telltales
Ribbons on the mainsail leech that should stream aft; the top one may stall half the time to windward. In: Rig and sails.
Leeward
(say "loo-ard") The side away from the wind. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Leeway
The small sideways slip of a boat through the water as it sails, a few degrees close-hauled. In: Sailing fundamentals.
Length overall, waterline length, beam
LOA is the hull from stem to stern (9.5–10.7 m for the boats on this page); LWL is the length at the waterline (the longer it is, the faster the boat can go); beam is the greatest width (3.1–3.5 m). In: Anatomy and terminology.
Lift-out and “on the hard”
Craning the boat ashore; a boat on the hard stands on props (supports), in a cradle or on a trailer. The only time the keel, rudder and skin fittings can be inspected properly. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Lift pump
A small pump on the engine that draws fuel from the tank and pushes it through the filters. Its hand priming lever is how you bleed the system. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Lip seal
A rubber sleeve on the stern tube with lips running on the shaft, as fitted by Volvo. Burp it after launch. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Lockers
Cupboards. Stowage anywhere you put things. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Log paddle wheel
A little paddle wheel in a through-hull fitting that measures speed through the water. It fouls quickly. In: Electronics.
Long keel
A keel that runs along most of the hull with the rudder hung at its back end. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Lower shrouds
Shorter shrouds from the base of the spreaders to the deck, one forward and one aft on each side, that stop the middle of the mast bending. From the side they make a narrow V. In: Anatomy and terminology, Rig and sails.
Luff
The front edge of a sail: on the mast for the main, on the forestay for the genoa. Its tension comes from the halyard. In: Rig and sails.
Luff, leech, foot
The three edges: front, back, bottom. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Luff telltales
Ribbons either side of the genoa near its luff that show whether the sail is at the right angle to the wind: both streaming is right. In: Rig and sails.
Luff up
Turn towards the wind; the same as head up. In: Sailing fundamentals.

M

Macerator
An electric pump that chops waste as it pumps it out of a holding tank. In: Boat systems.
Magnetic north
Where a compass free of the boat’s influence points. It differs from true north by the variation. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Main fuse
A large fuse close to each battery that protects the main cable. ABYC puts it within 178 mm of the battery. In: Boat systems.
Mainsail
The sail behind the mast, attached to the mast and the boom. In: Anatomy and terminology, Rig and sails.
Mainsheet
The rope-and-pulley system from the boom down to the traveller that pulls the mainsail in and lets it out. In: Anatomy and terminology, Rig and sails.
Mainsheet traveller
A slider on a track across the boat. Moving it changes where the mainsheet pulls from, so you can move the boom sideways without pulling it down. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Mandrel
The rod inside a furling mast or boom that the sail rolls around. In: Rig and sails.
Manual bilge pump
A big diaphragm pump worked from the cockpit, such as the Whale Gusher 10, about 65 litres a minute at a brisk pace. In: Boat systems.
Mast
The vertical pole (a spar) that holds the sails up. Anodised aluminium on this class. Deck-stepped masts sit on the coachroof; keel-stepped masts pass through the deck to the keel. In: Anatomy and terminology, Rig and sails.
Mast heel and step
The bottom of a keel-stepped mast and the fitting on the keel floors it stands on. In: Rig and sails.
Mast step plate
The plate on the coachroof that a deck-stepped mast stands in; halyards exit the mast just above it. In: Rig and sails.
Masthead
The top of the mast. It carries the pulleys (sheaves) the halyards run over, the wind instrument, the VHF radio aerial and usually an all-round white anchor light. Masthead / fractional rig
Where the forestay meets the mast: at the very top (masthead rig, most of this class) or part way up (fractional rig). In: Anatomy and terminology.
Masthead wind unit
A vane (for direction) and spinning cups (for speed) on a bracket at the top of the mast. In: Electronics.
Mechanical terminal
A screw-together end fitting (Sta-Lok, Norseman, Hi-Mod) that clamps the wire’s strands over a cone. Fitted with hand tools, reusable and inspectable. In: Rig and sails.
MHz and kHz
Megahertz and kilohertz: units of radio frequency. VHF marine radio is around 156 to 162 MHz; Navtex is 518 kHz TBC. In: Electronics.
Mid-tide
The middle two hours of the rise or fall, when the height changes fastest and the stream runs hardest. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Mixing valve
A thermostatic valve on the calorifier’s hot outlet that blends in cold water so the taps cannot scald. In: Boat systems.
MMSI
The boat’s nine-digit radio identity, issued with the ship radio licence; entered in the DSC VHF and AIS transponder. In: Electronics.
Moisture meter
An electronic meter held against the hull that indicates water in the laminate. Readings differ between makes; the comparison with a dry area of the same hull is what counts. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Mooring cleat
A horn cleat on the foredeck for mooring lines and the snubber; bolted through a backing plate because the loads are the highest on the deck. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Motor-sailing
Under sail with the engine driving the propeller: a power-driven vessel under the rules. By day, a black cone point down in the rigging. In: Navigation and passage planning.
MPPT controller
A solar controller that converts the panel’s best voltage to battery voltage, typically 20 to 30 % better than the simpler PWM type. In: Boat systems.
Multiplexer
A box that merges NMEA 0183 data from several talkers so one listener can hear them all. In: Electronics.

N

Nautical mile (NM)
1,852 m: one minute of latitude, which is why charts measure distance with the latitude scale. A knot is one nautical mile an hour. In: Electronics.
Navel pipe
The pipe or hole in the deck that the chain drops through into the locker; also called the spurling pipe. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Navtex
A receiver for coast stations’ printed warnings and forecasts on 518 kHz TBC, in English. In: Electronics.
Negative busbar
The bar where every negative cable joins; the start battery and house bank share it. In: Boat systems.
NMEA 2000 backbone
The single shared cable of an NMEA 2000 network that every device joins. In: Electronics.
No-go zone
The sector about 45° either side of the wind that no sailing boat can sail into; you tack across it. In: Sailing fundamentals.
Non-slip
The textured surface of the deck: a moulded pattern in the GRP, a rolled-on textured paint, or glued sheet. Worn smooth, it is renewed with paint. In: Deck hardware and steering.
North cardinal
Black over yellow, two cones pointing up, continuous quick flashes. Safe water is to its north. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Not under command
Unable to manoeuvre, for example with a broken engine or steering: two all-round red lights, two balls. In: Navigation and passage planning.

O

Oil analysis
A laboratory test of an oil sample that shows wear metals, fuel, soot or coolant in the oil. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Oil filler cap
The cap on the rocker cover where oil is added. Fill a little at a time and recheck the dipstick: too much is as bad as too little. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Oil filter
A spin-on canister that cleans the oil; changed with the oil. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Osmosis
Water passing through the gelcoat into the laminate and reacting with the resin to form blisters. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Outhaul
Tensions the bottom edge of the mainsail along the boom. In: Anatomy and terminology, Rig and sails.
Overboard seacock
The valve on the toilet’s discharge. Closed after use and whenever the boat is left. In: Boat systems.
Overlap
How far a genoa reaches past the mast, given as a percentage of J: 100% is a jib, 130 to 135% the usual cruising genoa, 150% a light-air sail. In: Rig and sails.

P

Painter
The rope on the front of the dinghy, the small rubber or rigid boat you row or motor ashore in. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Partners
The reinforced hole in the deck that a keel-stepped mast passes through, packed with wedges or Spartite and covered by a boot. In: Rig and sails.
Pawls
Small spring-loaded catches that let the drum turn one way and lock the other. Oil them, never grease them; replace the springs at every service. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Pedestal
The hollow column in the cockpit that carries the wheel, usually the compass and often the engine controls; the steering chain runs down inside it. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Pelican hook
The hinged hook with a sliding keeper that closes a gate in the guardrails. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Permeation
Water molecules passing slowly through a coating that is water-resistant but not waterproof. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Pickup
The pipe inside the tank that draws fuel, ending a little above the bottom so it misses the water and sludge, until a rough sea stirs them up. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Pilot vessel
On pilotage duty: white over red all-round lights; flag H by day. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Pintles and gudgeons
The pins and eyes that hinge a transom-hung rudder: the pintle is the pin, the gudgeon the eye it drops into. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Pivot point
The point the boat turns about: about a third of its length from the bow going ahead, further aft going astern. In: Manoeuvres under engine.
PLB
Personal locator beacon: a small satellite distress beacon registered to a person. In: Electronics.
Plough anchor
The CQR (with a hinged shank) and the one-piece Delta: the shape most boats of this age carry. Reliable, mediocre holding by modern standards. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Polarity light
A warning light that shows live and neutral have arrived swapped, common with Continental plugs. In: Boat systems.
Polyurethane sealant
Sikaflex 291 or 3M 5200: strong, near-permanent adhesive sealant. Use where you never want the joint apart, and not on acrylic (291 cracks it; 295 UV with primer is the glazing grade). In: Deck hardware and steering.
Pontoon
A floating jetty in a marina that boats tie up alongside. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Port
The left side when you face the bow. Its light at night is red. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Port-hand mark
Red, can-shaped or with a can topmark, red light: keep it on your port (left) side going with the direction of buoyage. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Port sidelight
A red light showing from dead ahead to 22.5° abaft the port beam. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Port tack
Sailing with the wind coming over the port side; the boom is out to starboard. In: Sailing fundamentals.
Pre-bend
A slight forward bow in the middle of the mast, set by the lower shrouds, so the mast cannot bow the wrong way under load. In: Rig and sails.
Preferred channel to port
Green with a broad red band, light Fl(2+1) G: the main channel is to port; treat it as a starboard-hand mark. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Preferred channel to starboard
Red with a broad green band, light Fl(2+1) R: the main channel is to starboard; treat it as a port-hand mark. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Pressure pump
An electric pump that starts when a tap opens and stops when it closes. In: Boat systems.
Preventer
A line from the end of the boom forward to the bow, to stop the boom slamming across in an accidental gybe when sailing downwind. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Primary filter and water separator
The first filter after the tank, usually with a clear bowl in which water settles and can be drained. Not a bleed point for the priming lever: after changing it, fill its bowl with clean fuel. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Print-through
The weave of the cloth showing faintly through the gelcoat. Cosmetic. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Prop walk
The sideways kick of the stern in astern, caused by the propeller. A right-handed propeller walks the stern to port. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Propane
The gas in red Calor cylinders and most Northern European ones; works down to about −40 °C. In: Boat systems.
Propeller
Pushes the boat by screwing through the water; described by diameter and pitch, in inches. Fixed, folding or feathering. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Propeller and shaft
The propeller sits under the hull, aft of the keel, on a shaft from the engine. Some boats use a saildrive instead: a gearbox leg that hangs straight down through the hull, like the lower half of an outboard motor. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Propeller hand
A right-handed propeller turns clockwise, seen from astern, going ahead; a left-handed one the other way. The hand sets the direction of prop walk. In: Manoeuvres under engine.
Propeller shaft
The stainless or bronze shaft from the coupling, through the stern gland, to the propeller. In: Engine and drivetrain.
PTFE
A slippery plastic (the coating on non-stick pans), used for stern-gland packing and tape. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Pulpit
The stainless-steel rail around the bow. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Pumping and inversion
Pumping: the middle of the mast flexing fore and aft in waves. Inversion: the mast bowing aft in the middle. Both mean the lowers or babystay are slack, and inversion can bring a fractional mast down. In: Rig and sails.
Pushpit
The stainless-steel rail around the stern. Also called the stern rail or, in American usage, the stern pulpit. In: Anatomy and terminology.

Q

Quadrant
The fan-shaped fitting on top of the stock that the steering cables pull on; on a tiller boat, the tiller head does the job. In: Hull, keel and rudder, Deck hardware and steering.
Quarter
The back corners: the port quarter and the starboard quarter. Something "on the quarter" is behind and to one side. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Quarter berth
A single berth running aft under the cockpit side, typical of 1970s–80s boats. Good sea berth, awkward to climb into. In: Anatomy and terminology.

R

Radar scanner
The rotating antenna of the radar, usually inside a dome on a bracket on the mast. In: Electronics.
Radio horizon
The distance at which an antenna’s signal meets the curve of the sea; two antennas can talk when their horizons meet. In: Electronics.
Rake
The whole mast leaning aft, one to two degrees on a cruising boat, which gives a little weather helm. In: Rig and sails.
Ram’s horn
The curved hook at the gooseneck that the luff reef cringle drops over when slab reefing. In: Rig and sails.
Raw-water pump and impeller
A belt- or gear-driven pump whose rubber impeller pushes sea water through the cooling system. Change the impeller every year; it dies quickly if run dry. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Raw-water strainer
A jar with a basket that catches weed and debris before the pump. The W of WOBBLE. In: Engine and drivetrain.
RCD
Residual current device: a switch that cuts the 230 V supply in a fraction of a second if current leaks to earth, as it would through a person. In: Boat systems.
“Ready about?”
The helm’s warning before a tack; the crew answer “Ready” when both jib sheets are prepared. In: Sailing fundamentals.
Reef
To make a sail smaller in strong wind. Slab reefing lowers the mainsail part way and ties the loose fold to the boom; furling rolls a sail around the furler tube on the forestay, or inside the mast or boom. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Reef bundle
The slab of sail below a reef, folded onto the boom and tied loosely with the reef points. In: Rig and sails.
Reef cringles and points
Reinforced eyes at luff and leech for each reef, with short ties (reef points) between them to bundle the spare sail. In: Rig and sails.
Reefing line
The line from the end of the boom up through the leech reef cringle and back down, which pulls the back of the sail down to the boom. In: Rig and sails.
Reefing lines
Lines that pull the reinforced eyes (cringles) in the sail down to the boom when you reef. The small ties that gather the loose fold are the reef points. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Regulator
The valve on the cylinder that reduces its pressure to the cooker’s: 28 or 37 mbar in the UK tradition, 30 mbar for Euro regulators. In: Boat systems.
Reinforced hose
Wire- or fabric-reinforced hose that will not kink or collapse and is rated for below-waterline use. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Resin
The liquid plastic that soaks the glass and sets hard. Polyester on all boats of this class; the cheap orthophthalic type until the 1980s, the more water-resistant isophthalic type afterwards; vinylester and epoxy are better still and dearer. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Restricted in ability to manoeuvre
Doing work that stops her getting out of the way, such as dredging or laying cable: red, white, red lights; ball, diamond, ball. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Return (leak-off) line
A small pipe that carries the fuel the injectors did not use back to the tank. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Rigging screw
The threaded barrel at the bottom of each wire that tensions it: a bottlescrew in Britain, a turnbuckle in America. Locked with split pins or wire. In: Rig and sails.
Rigging screws (turnbuckles)
Threaded fittings at the bottom of each wire for tensioning it. Locked with split pins or wire. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Roach
The curved extra area of the mainsail's back edge beyond a straight line from head to clew. In: Anatomy and terminology, Rig and sails.
Rope clutch
A lever-operated jaw that holds a loaded line so that the winch can be used for another. In: Rig and sails, Deck hardware and steering.
Rudder bearings
The bushes the stock turns in: plastic or roller. Play here is felt as a clonk at the wheel. In: Hull, keel and rudder, Deck hardware and steering, Buying and owning.
Rudder stock
The shaft the rudder blade turns on, stainless steel or aluminium. In: Hull, keel and rudder, Deck hardware and steering.
Rudder stops
Blocks either side of the quadrant that limit the rudder’s swing before it hits the hull or the chain runs off the sprocket. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Rudder tube
The tube moulded into the hull that the rudder stock passes through, with a bearing at the top and one at the bottom. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Run
The wind dead astern, the sails right out; the boat rolls and the risk of an accidental gybe is highest. In: Sailing fundamentals.
Running backstays and checkstays
Removable backstays, set up on the windward side and let go on each tack, that support a fractional mast from behind; checkstays are lighter ones to the middle of the mast that stop pumping. Racing gear, rare on this fleet. In: Rig and sails.

S

Safe water mark
Red and white vertical stripes, one red ball, a white light that is isophase, occulting, one long flash every 10 seconds or Morse A: safe water all round, often the start of a channel. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Sail cloth
Woven polyester (Dacron) on almost all cruising boats of this class. Laminates are lighter and hold shape but die faster in the sun. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Sail cover
Canvas over the furled mainsail on the boom. Sunlight is what kills sails; the cover greatly extends their life. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Saildrive anode
A block or ring of a more easily corroded metal on the leg, which corrodes so the aluminium leg does not. Renew before it is half gone. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Saildrive diaphragm
The thick rubber seal between the saildrive leg and the hull. Volvo Penta says renew it every seven years. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Saildrive leg
An aluminium leg with the gearbox and a right-angle drive, bolted under the engine and through the hull. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Saloon
The main cabin with seating and a table. The settees usually double as sea berths. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Saloon table
Fixed or folding, on the centreline or against one settee. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Scoop (new-generation) anchor
Spade (1996), Rocna (2004), Mantus, Ultra and others: a concave fluke, often with a roll bar, that digs in at once and holds far more per kilogram. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Scope
The ratio of chain out to depth, counting the height of the bow. Commonly given as four or five to one in settled weather ² and six or more in strong wind; more scope means a flatter pull and a better-set anchor. Yachting Monthly’s version: metres of chain equal to knots of wind plus metres of boat. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Sea breeze
A daytime onshore wind caused by the land heating up; it starts late in the morning and fades at dusk. In: Seas and cruising grounds.
Seacock
The valve on a through-hull that lets you shut it. Learn where every one is. In: Anatomy and terminology, Hull, keel and rudder.
Sealant bed
The layer of sealant under a fitting, with a countersunk ring around each bolt that stays put when the fitting is tightened. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Self-tailing jaws
Two sprung discs on top of a winch that grip the rope’s tail, so one person can wind with both hands. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Settee / sea berth
The bench seats along each side of the saloon. With a lee cloth (a canvas side that stops you rolling out) rigged, the safest place to sleep at sea. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Shackle and block
A shackle is a U-shaped metal link with a pin, for joining things. A block is a pulley. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Shaft coupling
The joint between the gearbox and the shaft, often a flexible one. Alignment is checked here. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Sheer
The curve of the deck edge seen from the side, usually lowest a little behind the middle. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Sheet
A line that controls a sail's angle to the wind. Not the sail itself. The mainsheet and the two genoa sheets are the lines you touch most. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Shore-power inlet
The socket on the boat that the shore lead plugs into. In: Boat systems.
Shrouds
The wires that hold the mast up sideways. Without them the mast would fall over the side. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Shunt
A precise resistor in the house bank’s negative cable that the battery monitor measures every amp through. In: Boat systems.
Side deck
The walkway between the coachroof and the edge of the deck. Narrow side decks make going forward awkward and wet. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Sidelights
Red to port, green to starboard, on the bow pulpit, each showing from dead ahead to just behind the beam. In: Electronics.
Skeg
A fixed fin ahead of the rudder that supports it. A spade rudder has no skeg and stands on its stock alone. In: Anatomy and terminology, Hull, keel and rudder.
Skeg-hung rudder
A rudder supported at the bottom by a fixed fin, the skeg. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Skin fitting
A threaded tube with a flange that passes through the hull; the outside half of a through-hull. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Sliding hatch
The lid over the companionway that slides forward to open. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Snubber
A short, stretchy nylon rope from a hook on the chain to a cleat, taking the load off the windlass and stopping the chain snatching in a swell. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Softwood bung
A tapered wooden plug that can be hammered into a broken fitting to stop a leak. One tied to each seacock. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Solar panel
Panels on the sprayhood, a frame or the deck. Shade on part of one cuts most of its output. In: Boat systems.
Sole
The cabin floor. Deckhead the ceiling. Bulkhead an internal wall. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Solenoid valve
An electric valve at the locker, switched from the galley, that turns the gas off at source; the gas alarm closes it. In: Boat systems.
South cardinal
Yellow over black, two cones pointing down, six quick flashes and a long flash. Safe water is to its south. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Spade rudder
A rudder blade held only by its stock. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Special mark
Yellow, a yellow X, a yellow light: marks something that is not a navigation hazard, such as an outfall, a zone or a data buoy. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Speed over the ground
How far you move over the seabed in an hour: the distance along the ground track to where the water track cuts it. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Spinnaker / cruising chute / gennaker
Large, light, colourful sails for sailing downwind. Fun, occasionally chaotic. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Spinnaker pole / whisker pole
A spar that holds the outer corner of a downwind sail away from the boat, on the side opposite the mainsail. Its own lines, the uphaul and downhaul, hold it up and down. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Split charging (VSR or DC-DC charger)
A device that lets the alternator charge both banks without joining them for good: a voltage-sensing relay joins them while charging; a DC-DC charger charges the house bank at its own voltage. In: Boat systems.
Split pin
The bent wire pin (a cotter pin) that stops a clevis pin walking out. The smallest part of the rig and the one that keeps the mast up. Never re-use one. In: Rig and sails.
Splitter
A box that lets the VHF and the AIS share one antenna. In: Electronics.
Sprayhood
The folding canvas hood over the companionway. Keeps spray out of the cabin and off the helm. Americans call it a dodger; in Britain dodgers are the cloth panels on the guardrails. In: Anatomy and terminology, Deck hardware and steering.
Spreader root
Where the spreader meets the mast. It must not move; cracks in the bracket and play in the root are common causes of rig failure. In: Rig and sails.
Spreader tip
The outer end of the spreader, where the cap shroud is clamped or seized to it and covered with a boot so the genoa does not chafe. In: Rig and sails.
Spreaders
Horizontal struts part way up the mast that push the cap shrouds out, so the wires pull at a better angle. In: Anatomy and terminology, Rig and sails.
Spring from the bow
A line from the bow leading aft to the pontoon; it stops the boat moving forward. Used to spring the stern out. In: Manoeuvres under engine.
Spring from the stern
A line from the stern leading forward to the pontoon; it stops the boat moving back. Used to spring the bow out. In: Manoeuvres under engine.
Spring tide and neap tide
The largest and smallest tidal ranges of the fortnightly cycle, around full and new moon and around the half moons. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Sprocket and chain
The toothed wheel on the wheel’s axle and the bicycle-type chain that runs over it, joining the wheel to the two steering cables. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Stanchion
A post along the deck edge, about 600 mm tall, carrying the guardrail wires. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Stanchion base
The foot of a stanchion, bolted through the deck with a backing plate. Levered by every body that falls against the rail, so the commonest deck leak. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Stanchion base leak
Water past a levered, cracked base into the deck core: soft deck and stained lining below. In: Deck hardware and steering.
“Stand by to gybe”
The helm’s warning before a gybe: heads clear of the boom, preventer off. In: Sailing fundamentals.
Starboard
The right side when you face the bow. Green light. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Starboard-hand mark
Green, cone-shaped or with a cone topmark pointing up, green light: keep it on your starboard side going with the direction of buoyage. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Starboard sidelight
A green light showing from dead ahead to 22.5° abaft the starboard beam. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Starboard tack
Sailing with the wind coming over the starboard side; the boom is out to port. Against a boat on port tack it is the stand-on vessel: it holds its course and speed, and acts only if the other does not. In: Sailing fundamentals.
Start battery
A battery kept only for starting the engine, so that the engine starts whatever the house bank has been through. In: Boat systems.
Starter motor
The electric motor that spins the engine until it fires. Slow cranking is nearly always the battery or its cables, not the starter. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Steaming light
A white light on the front of the mast, lit when the boat is motoring. In: Electronics, Navigation and passage planning.
Steering cables
The two wires, one from each end of the chain, that run to the two ends of the quadrant. Edson says replace them every five years. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Stem
The leading edge of the bow, where the two sides of the hull meet. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Stern
The back end. Aft is towards it; astern is behind the boat. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Stern anchor
A kedge anchor dropped astern on the approach, often on a reel of webbing tape at the pushpit, that holds the boat off the rock. In: Seas and cruising grounds.
Stern gland
The seal where the propeller shaft leaves the hull. It is allowed to drip a little; more than that sinks boats slowly. In: Anatomy and terminology, Engine and drivetrain.
Stern line
A mooring line from the stern aft to the pontoon or quay: it holds the stern in and stops the boat moving forward. In: Manoeuvres under engine.
Stern spring, doubled back
The stern spring led round the pontoon cleat and back on board, ready to slip. In: Manoeuvres under engine.
Stern swing
When the boat turns ahead, the stern swings out much further than the bow swings in. In: Manoeuvres under engine.
Stern tube
The tube moulded into the hull that the propeller shaft runs through; the stern gland is clamped to its inner end. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Sternlight
A white light at the stern, showing astern and a little either side; shown with the sidelights, or replaced by the tricolour under sail. In: Electronics, Navigation and passage planning.
Stop control
The lever on the injection pump that cuts the fuel off, worked by a pull knob at the helm (out to stop, back in to run) or by a solenoid from a stop button or the key. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Storm jib and trysail
A small, flat, heavy headsail and a small mainsail-substitute on its own track, for gales. Sized by rule of thumb from I and from P and E. In: Rig and sails.
Stray current
Electricity leaking into the water from faulty wiring on the boat or in the marina. It eats metal fittings far faster than ordinary galvanic corrosion. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Stringers
Lengthwise ribs bonded inside the hull to stiffen the skin; floors run across, stringers run fore and aft. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Strum box
A strainer on the end of a bilge pump’s suction hose that keeps debris out of the pump. In: Boat systems.
Sulphation
Hard crystals that grow on the plates of a lead-acid battery left discharged. They cut its capacity for good. In: Boat systems.
Sump
The oil pan at the bottom of the engine. The oil lives here; the dipstick reads its level. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Survey
A professional inspection of the boat out of the water, with a written report. Always get one before buying a boat of this age. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Swage cracks and meat hooks
The two visible signs of a dying terminal: hairline cracks running down the sleeve, and broken strands standing proud where the wire enters. In: Rig and sails.
Swaged terminal
A stainless sleeve squeezed onto the end of a wire by a rigger’s machine. Strong, neat, and impossible to inspect inside. In: Rig and sails.

T

T-piece and drop cable
The connector on the backbone and the short cable from it to each device. In: Electronics.
T-terminal and tang
A T-shaped fitting (also called a T-ball) on the mast end of a shroud that hooks into a slot in the mast wall; the older alternative is a tang, a strap riveted to the mast that the shroud pins to. Check for wear in the slot and cracking at the neck. In: Rig and sails.
Tack and gybe (the turns)
Turning the boat so that the wind crosses from one side to the other. Turning the bow through the wind is a tack; turning the stern through it, with the wind behind you, is a gybe. Both make the boom swing across. See Sailing fundamentals. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Tackle
A rope led back and forth through two blocks (pulleys) so that one pull moves the load with several times the force: the mainsheet, the kicker and a backstay adjuster are all tackles. In: Rig and sails.
Tank drain seacock
The seacock under the holding tank for emptying it at sea. In: Boat systems.
Tank vent
A small pipe from the tank to a swan-neck outlet on the hull side, letting air in as fuel is used. Blocked, the engine starves; badly placed, it lets sea water in. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Tanks and batteries
Fresh water and diesel tanks sit low under the berths or the cabin floor; the batteries live in a ventilated box near the engine. Know where each is and how much it holds. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Taps
Mixer taps at the galley and basin, fed by the cold and hot lines; a foot pump often sits beside the galley one. In: Boat systems.
Teak deck
Teak planks laid over the GRP deck, screwed and glued on older boats. Beautiful, and the most expensive thing on the boat to replace when the planks wear through. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Telltales
Ribbons on the sail that show which way the air is flowing over it. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Thermostat
A valve that keeps the coolant in the engine until it is warm, then lets it through the heat exchanger. Stuck shut, the engine overheats; stuck open, it runs cold. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Through-bolt
A bolt passing right through the deck to a nut and backing plate below. Screws into the deck skin alone pull out. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Through-hull / skin fitting
Any hole through the hull with a fitting: engine cooling water inlet, sink drains, the speed and depth sensors. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Tidal stream vector
One hour of tidal stream, direction and rate, drawn with three arrowheads. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Tide curve
The rise of the tide from low to high water: slow at first, fastest at mid-tide, slow again near high water. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Tiller
A lever fixed straight onto the rudder stock, the simplest steering there is. In: Hull, keel and rudder, Deck hardware and steering.
Toe rail
The raised strip along the deck edge, often slotted aluminium, that stops your feet sliding off and gives you somewhere to attach blocks (pulleys). In: Anatomy and terminology, Deck hardware and steering.
Toggle
A small hinge between the rigging screw and the chainplate that lets the wire swing so it is never bent at its terminal. In: Rig and sails.
Toilet bowl
On most boats of this size it sits below the waterline, which is why its hoses need vented loops. In: Boat systems.
Topping lift
A line from the masthead to the end of the boom that holds the boom up when the sail is down. In: Anatomy and terminology, Rig and sails.
Topsides
The hull above the waterline. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Torque tube
The rotating rod or tube that joins the gearboxes of a rod steering system. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Transit
Two fixed objects seen in line; if they stop lining up while at anchor, the anchor is dragging. In: Manoeuvres under engine.
Transom
The flat or near-flat surface across the stern. The bathing platform and boarding ladder are on or below it. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Transom-hung rudder
A rudder hung on the outside of the stern on pintles and gudgeons. In: Hull, keel and rudder.
Traveller
A track across the cockpit or coachroof that the mainsheet block slides along, moving the boom sideways without pulling it down. In: Rig and sails.
Trawling
Dragging a net: green over white all-round lights, two cones point to point. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Tricolour
Red, green and white sectors in one masthead lantern, allowed for a sailing vessel under 20 m under sail only. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Tricolour and anchor light
A masthead light combining red, green and white sectors, for use under sail only, usually with an all-round white anchor light in the same housing. In: Electronics.
True north
The direction of the geographic north pole; chart grids and chart bearings use it. In: Navigation and passage planning.
True wind
The wind as it would be felt by a boat drifting with the water; the instruments calculate it from the apparent wind and the speed through the water. In: Electronics, Sailing fundamentals.
TSS
Traffic separation scheme: one-way lanes for ships, like a dual carriageway at sea. In: Navigation and passage planning.

U

UV strip
A sacrificial strip of acrylic or polyester along the leech and foot of a furling genoa that faces the sun when the sail is rolled. The first part of the sail to rot. In: Rig and sails.

V

V-berth
The two berths in the forecabin that meet at the bow, often with an infill cushion to make a double. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Variation
The angle between true and magnetic north at a place, printed on the chart with its year and yearly change. West or east. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Vented loop
A loop of hose high above the waterline with a small valve at the top that breaks any siphon. In: Boat systems.
VHF antenna
The whip antenna at the masthead. The higher it is, the further the VHF reaches. In: Electronics.
Voltage drop
The voltage lost along a cable; too much and equipment misbehaves. ABYC allows 3 % for important circuits. In: Boat systems.

W

Warp
A mooring or anchoring rope. Bow line, stern line and two springs (diagonal lines that stop the boat surging forwards and backwards) are the standard set of four. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Washboards
Removable boards that close the companionway opening in bad weather. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Water and sludge
What settles in the bottom of an old tank: water, rust and diesel bug. Heavy weather stirs it into the filters. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Water filler
The deck fitting for filling the water tank, marked WATER. Read it before the fuel nozzle goes in. In: Boat systems.
Water strainer
A small filter before the pressure pump that catches debris from the tank. In: Boat systems.
Water tank
Stainless, plastic or a flexible bladder; 100 to 570 litres on the reference boats. In: Boat systems.
Water track
The course to steer through the water, drawn with one arrowhead; its length is one hour of boat speed. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Waterline
Where the hull meets the water at rest. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Waterlock
A box low in the exhaust run that holds the water in the hose when the engine stops, so it cannot run back into the engine. In: Engine and drivetrain.
Weather helm
The boat’s tendency to turn into the wind, felt as a steady pull on the helm. A little is normal; hydraulic steering hides it. In: Deck hardware and steering, Sailing fundamentals.
West cardinal
Yellow, black, yellow, two cones point to point, nine quick flashes. Safe water is to its west. In: Navigation and passage planning.
Wheel
Turns the rudder through a mechanism: chain and cable, rod or hydraulics. Mounted on a pedestal. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Wheel or tiller
What you steer with. A wheel stands on a pedestal in the cockpit; a tiller is a lever fixed straight onto the rudder stock. Every wheel-steered boat should also carry an emergency tiller for when the wheel gear fails; check that it is aboard. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Wheelhouse
The enclosed, glazed deckhouse of a motorsailer (a boat built to motor as much as to sail), with an inside steering position and often a table with seats (a dinette). In: Anatomy and terminology.
Winch
A drum you wind a rope around so you can pull far harder than by hand. Self-tailing winches grip the rope for you. The largest, on the coamings, are the primaries for the genoa sheets. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Winch drum
The barrel the rope is wound round, three or four turns. Its size number is roughly the pull in kilograms for one kilogram on the handle. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Winch gears
The gearing inside the base that multiplies the handle’s turns; two-speed winches change gear when you wind the other way. In: Deck hardware and steering.
Windlass
The winch, manual or electric, that hauls the anchor chain in over the bow roller. Many older boats have none, and the chain comes up by hand. In: Anatomy and terminology, Deck hardware and steering.
Windward
The side the wind is coming from. Also called the weather side. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Working sheet / lazy sheet
The genoa has two sheets, one each side. The one under load is the working sheet; the slack one is the lazy sheet. They swap every tack. In: Anatomy and terminology.
Woven roving
A heavy woven glass cloth laid between layers of mat for strength. In: Hull, keel and rudder.

Y

Y-valve
A two-way valve that sends the toilet’s waste to the holding tank or straight overboard. In: Boat systems.
Yachtmaster Coastal
An exam, with minimum sea time, showing competence to skipper on coastal passages. In: Licences and qualifications.
Yachtmaster Offshore
An exam, with more sea time and passages, showing competence to skipper offshore. In: Licences and qualifications.
