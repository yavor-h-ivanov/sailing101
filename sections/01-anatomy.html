Anatomy and terminology

Sailing has its own vocabulary because precision matters when someone shouts an instruction across a windy cockpit. Learn these words first; everything else on this page uses them.

The boat in profile

A typical masthead sloop of the class, seen from the side with the bow to the right. Hover over or read the labels; every term is explained in the lists below.

Directions and positions

  • Bow The front end. Forward is towards it.
  • Stern The back end. Aft is towards it; astern is behind the boat.
  • Port The left side when facing forward. Red light, red buoys in Europe (IALA A) when entering harbour.
  • Starboard The right side when facing forward. Green light.
  • Amidships The middle of the boat, lengthwise.
  • Windward The side the wind is coming from. Also called the weather side.
  • Leeward (say "loo-ard") The side away from the wind.
  • Ahead / astern In front of / behind the boat. Also the two gear directions of the engine.
  • Abeam At right angles to the boat's centreline.
  • Below Inside the cabin. On deck is outside.

Hull and appendages

  • Hull The watertight body of the boat. On this class, glass-reinforced plastic (GRP, "fibreglass").
  • Topsides The hull above the waterline.
  • Waterline Where the hull meets the water at rest. The painted line is the boot top.
  • Antifouling Toxic or slippery paint below the waterline that slows marine growth.
  • Keel The fixed fin under the hull that provides ballast (righting force) and lateral resistance. Fin, bilge (twin) or long keel: see Hull, keel and rudder.
  • Rudder The steerable foil at the stern. Turned by a tiller or a wheel via the rudder stock.
  • Skeg A fixed fin ahead of the rudder that supports it. A spade rudder has no skeg.
  • Transom The flat or near-flat surface across the stern.
  • Stem The leading edge of the bow.
  • Sheer The curve of the deck edge seen from the side.
  • Freeboard Height of the deck above the water.
  • Draught (draft) Depth from waterline to the bottom of the keel. Decides where you can float.
  • Displacement The boat's mass, equal to the water it displaces. Around 4–7 tonnes for this class TBC per boat.
  • Through-hull / skin fitting Any hole through the hull with a fitting: engine intake, sink drains, log and depth sensors.
  • Seacock The valve on a through-hull that lets you shut it. Learn where every one is.

Deck and cockpit

  • Coachroof The raised cabin top. Its front is the coachroof front; the side windows are coachroof windows.
  • Cockpit The sunken working area where the crew sits and steers.
  • Centre cockpit / aft cockpit Where the cockpit sits. Centre cockpit (Moody 33) buys an aft cabin; aft cockpit (Sadler 32, Bavaria 1060) is more common.
  • Coaming The raised edge around the cockpit that keeps water out and gives you a backrest.
  • Companionway The steps and hatch from the cockpit down into the cabin.
  • Washboards Removable boards that close the companionway in bad weather.
  • Sprayhood (dodger) The folding canvas hood over the companionway.
  • Bimini A sun awning over the cockpit. Common on Mediterranean boats.
  • Pulpit The stainless rail at the bow. Pushpit the one at the stern.
  • Stanchions and guardrails The vertical posts and wires along the deck edge. Also called lifelines.
  • Toe rail The raised strip along the deck edge (often slotted aluminium).
  • Cleat A fitting for securing a rope. Fairlead a fitting that guides a rope without securing it.
  • Winch A drum you wind a rope around to pull harder. Self-tailing winches grip the rope for you.
  • Clutch / jammer A lever that grips a rope so you can take it off the winch.
  • Traveller A car on a track that lets you move where a sheet pulls from.
  • Hatch Any opening in the deck with a lid: forehatch, cockpit locker, lazarette (stern locker).
  • Anchor locker The bow locker holding the anchor chain. Its winch is the windlass.
  • Bathing platform / boarding ladder Stern access to the water. Standard on the Bavaria, rare on the older British boats.

Rig: spars and standing rigging

The rig is everything that holds the sails up. Standing rigging is fixed wire (or rod) that keeps the mast up. Running rigging is rope you adjust while sailing.

  • Mast The vertical spar. Usually anodised aluminium on this class. Deck-stepped sits on the coachroof; keel-stepped passes through the deck to the keel.
  • Boom The horizontal spar along the foot of the mainsail. It swings. It hurts.
  • Gooseneck The universal joint attaching the boom to the mast.
  • Masthead / fractional Where the forestay meets the mast: at the top (masthead rig, most of this class) or part way up (fractional rig).
  • Forestay Wire from masthead to bow. Holds the mast from falling aft; carries the headsail (often inside a furler).
  • Backstay Wire from masthead to stern. A split backstay forks to both quarters; an adjuster lets you tension it.
  • Shrouds Wires from the mast to the deck edge on each side: cap shrouds to the masthead, lowers to the spreader roots.
  • Spreaders Horizontal struts that push the cap shrouds out for a better angle.
  • Chainplates The metal straps on or through the deck that the shrouds attach to. A classic leak and corrosion point.
  • Rigging screws (turnbuckles) Threaded fittings at the bottom of each wire for tensioning.
  • Spinnaker pole / whisker pole A spar that holds a downwind sail's clew out.
  • Kicker (vang) A strut or tackle from the mast foot to the boom that holds the boom down. A rod kicker also holds it up when the sail is down.
  • Topping lift A line from the masthead to the boom end that holds the boom up when the sail is down.

Sails and their parts

  • Mainsail The sail behind the mast, attached to mast and boom.
  • Headsail Any sail forward of the mast. A genoa overlaps the mast; a jib does not. A storm jib is tiny and heavy.
  • Spinnaker / cruising chute / gennaker Large light downwind sails. Fun, occasionally chaotic.
  • Head, tack, clew The three corners: top, front-bottom, back-bottom.
  • Luff, leech, foot The three edges: front, back, bottom.
  • Battens Stiffeners in pockets along the mainsail leech.
  • Roach The curved extra area of the leech beyond a straight line from head to clew.
  • Telltales Ribbons on the sail that show airflow.
  • Reef To reduce sail area. Slab reefing pulls the main down to reef points; furling rolls a sail around a foil or inside the mast or boom.
  • Sail cloth Woven polyester (Dacron) on almost all cruising boats of this class. Laminates are lighter and hold shape but die faster in the sun.

Running rigging: the ropes

  • Halyard Hoists a sail. Main halyard, genoa halyard, spinnaker halyard.
  • Sheet Controls a sail's angle to the wind. The mainsheet and two genoa sheets are the ropes you touch most.
  • Outhaul Tensions the foot of the mainsail along the boom.
  • Cunningham Tensions the mainsail luff from the tack.
  • Reefing lines Pull the reef points down to the boom.
  • Furling line Rolls the headsail furler.
  • Guy Controls a spinnaker pole.
  • Preventer A line from the boom end forward to stop an accidental gybe.
  • Warp A mooring or anchoring rope. Bow line, stern line, springs are the standard set of four.
  • Painter The rope on the dinghy.

Below decks

  • Saloon The main cabin with seating and table.
  • Galley The kitchen. Cooker on gimbals swings level as the boat heels.
  • Heads The toilet compartment, and the toilet itself.
  • Forepeak / forecabin The bow cabin. Aft cabin the stern cabin.
  • Quarter berth A single berth tucked under the cockpit side.
  • Chart table / nav station Where the charts, instruments and switch panel live.
  • Bilge The lowest inside part of the hull, where water collects. The bilge pump empties it.
  • Sole The cabin floor. Deckhead the ceiling. Bulkhead an internal wall.
  • Lockers Cupboards. Stowage anywhere you put things.
  • Holding tank Stores toilet waste for pumping out ashore. Required in many of the seas covered here.
