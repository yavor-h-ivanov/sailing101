Anatomy and terminology

Sailing has its own vocabulary because precision matters when someone shouts an instruction across a windy cockpit. Learn these words first; everything else on this page uses them. Every label in the diagrams is linked to its definition, and every defined term can be found back on a diagram.

The boat in profile

A typical masthead sloop of the class seen from the side, bow to the right, sails hoisted. This is the view you will see in every brochure and the one most terms are easiest to learn from.

Directions and positions

  • Bow The front end. Forward is towards it.
  • Stern The back end. Aft is towards it; astern is behind the boat.
  • Port The left side when facing forward. Red light at night. In European waters (IALA region A) the red buoys are left to port when entering harbour.
  • Starboard The right side when facing forward. Green light.
  • Amidships The middle of the boat, lengthwise.
  • Windward The side the wind is coming from. Also called the weather side.
  • Leeward (say "loo-ard") The side away from the wind.
  • Ahead / astern In front of / behind the boat. Also the two gear directions of the engine.
  • Abeam At right angles to the boat's centreline.
  • Below Inside the cabin. On deck is outside.

Hull and underwater parts

  • Hull The watertight body of the boat. On this class, glass-reinforced plastic (GRP, "fibreglass").
  • Topsides The hull above the waterline.
  • Waterline Where the hull meets the water at rest. The painted stripe just above it is the boot top.
  • Antifouling Paint below the waterline that slows weed and barnacle growth.
  • Keel The fixed fin under the hull. Its ballast rights the boat and its area stops it sliding sideways. Fin, bilge (twin) or long keel: see Hull, keel and rudder.
  • Rudder The steerable foil at the stern. Turned by a tiller or a wheel through the rudder stock.
  • Skeg A fixed fin ahead of the rudder that supports it. A spade rudder has no skeg.
  • Propeller and shaft The propeller sits under the hull, aft of the keel, on a shaft from the engine or on a saildrive leg.
  • Transom The flat or near-flat surface across the stern.
  • Stem The leading edge of the bow.
  • Sheer The curve of the deck edge seen from the side.
  • Freeboard Height of the deck above the water.
  • Draught (draft) Depth from waterline to the bottom of the keel. Decides where you can float.
  • Displacement The boat's mass, equal to the water it displaces. About 4 to 7 tonnes for this class.
  • Through-hull / skin fitting Any hole through the hull with a fitting: engine intake, sink drains, log and depth sensors.
  • Seacock The valve on a through-hull that lets you shut it. Learn where every one is.

The deck from above

The same boat with the sails down and the boom stowed on the centreline. This is where the working gear lives, and where you will spend most of your time when berthing.

Deck and cockpit

  • Coachroof The raised cabin top, with the coachroof windows in its sides. Also called the cabin top.
  • Side deck The walkway between the coachroof and the edge of the deck. Narrow side decks are a real annoyance.
  • Foredeck The deck forward of the mast, where the anchor and the headsail are handled.
  • Cockpit The sunken working area where the crew sits and steers.
  • Centre cockpit / aft cockpit Where the cockpit sits. Centre cockpit (Moody 33) buys an aft cabin; aft cockpit (Sadler 32, Bavaria 1060) is more common.
  • Wheel or tiller What you steer with. A wheel stands on a pedestal in the cockpit; a tiller is a lever straight onto the rudder stock. Both turn the same rudder.
  • Coaming The raised edge around the cockpit that keeps water out and gives you a backrest. The primary winches usually sit on it.
  • Companionway The steps and opening from the cockpit down into the cabin, closed by a sliding hatch on top and washboards in front.
  • Sliding hatch The hatch over the companionway that slides forward to open.
  • Washboards Removable boards that close the companionway in bad weather.
  • Sprayhood (dodger) The folding canvas hood over the companionway. Keeps spray out of the cabin and the helm.
  • Bimini A sun awning over the cockpit. Common on Mediterranean boats.
  • Pulpit The stainless-steel rail around the bow.
  • Pushpit The stainless-steel rail around the stern. Also called the stern rail or, in American usage, the stern pulpit.
  • Guardrails and stanchions The wires along the deck edge (also called lifelines) and the vertical posts that hold them up.
  • Toe rail The raised strip along the deck edge, often slotted aluminium, that stops your feet sliding off and gives you somewhere to attach blocks.
  • Cleat A fitting for securing a rope. There are usually two at the bow, two amidships and two at the stern.
  • Fairlead A fitting that guides a rope over the deck edge without chafing, without securing it.
  • Winch A drum you wind a rope around to pull harder. Self-tailing winches grip the rope for you. The largest ones, on the coamings, are the primaries for the genoa sheets.
  • Halyard winches Smaller winches on the coachroof (or on the mast on older boats) for hoisting and reefing.
  • Clutch (jammer) A lever that grips a rope so you can take it off the winch and use the winch for another line.
  • Mainsheet traveller A car on a track across the boat that changes where the mainsheet pulls from, so you can control the boom's angle and the sail's twist separately.
  • Genoa track and car A track along each side deck with a sliding block (the car) that sets the angle the genoa sheet leads at.
  • Chainplate The metal strap on or through the deck that a shroud attaches to. A classic leak and corrosion point.
  • Hatch Any opening in the deck with a lid. The forehatch on the foredeck doubles as the emergency exit from the forecabin.
  • Forehatch The hatch on the foredeck over the forecabin.
  • Dorade vent A cowl vent with a box below it that lets air in and drains water out. Named after the yacht that first carried them.
  • Cockpit locker Deep stowage under the cockpit seats for fenders, warps and everything else.
  • Lazarette The stern locker, right aft, often holding the gas bottles or the steering gear.
  • Anchor locker The bow locker holding the anchor chain. The electric winch that hauls it is the windlass.
  • Windlass The winch, manual or electric, that hauls the anchor chain over the bow roller.
  • Bow roller The fitting at the stem over which the anchor chain runs, and where the anchor usually lives.
  • Bathing platform / boarding ladder Stern access to the water. Standard on the Bavaria, rare on the older British boats.

The rig seen from ahead

Looking at the boat from directly in front shows what the profile hides: the mast is held up sideways by the shrouds, spread out by the spreaders and anchored to the hull at the chainplates.

Rig: spars and standing rigging

The rig is everything that holds the sails up. Standing rigging is fixed wire (or rod) that keeps the mast up. Running rigging is rope you adjust while sailing.

  • Mast The vertical spar. Usually anodised aluminium on this class. Deck-stepped sits on the coachroof; keel-stepped passes through the deck to the keel.
  • Masthead The top of the mast, carrying the halyard sheaves, the navigation light, the wind instrument and the VHF aerial.
  • Boom The horizontal spar along the foot of the mainsail. It swings. It hurts.
  • Gooseneck The universal joint attaching the boom to the mast.
  • Masthead / fractional rig Where the forestay meets the mast: at the top (masthead rig, most of this class) or part way up (fractional rig).
  • Forestay Wire from the masthead to the bow. Stops the mast falling aft and carries the headsail, usually inside a furler foil.
  • Furler A drum at the bottom of the forestay and a foil around it: pull the furling line and the genoa rolls up around the foil.
  • Backstay Wire from the masthead to the stern. A split backstay forks to both quarters; an adjuster lets you tension it.
  • Shrouds The wires that hold the mast up sideways.
  • Cap shrouds The shrouds that run from the masthead over the spreader tips to the chainplates.
  • Lower shrouds Shorter shrouds from the spreader roots to the deck, usually one forward and one aft on each side, which stop the middle of the mast bending.
  • Spreaders Horizontal struts that push the cap shrouds out for a better angle at the masthead.
  • Chainplates The metal straps that anchor the shrouds to the hull or deck.
  • Rigging screws (turnbuckles) Threaded fittings at the bottom of each wire for tensioning. Locked with split pins or wire.
  • Spinnaker pole / whisker pole A spar that holds a downwind sail's clew out.
  • Kicker (vang) A strut or tackle from the mast foot to the boom that holds the boom down. A rod kicker also holds it up when the sail is down.
  • Topping lift A line from the masthead to the boom end that holds the boom up when the sail is down.

Sails and their parts

  • Mainsail The sail behind the mast, attached to the mast and the boom.
  • Headsail Any sail forward of the mast. A genoa overlaps the mast; a jib does not. A storm jib is tiny and heavy.
  • Genoa A large headsail whose clew reaches aft of the mast. Sized by overlap: a "135% genoa" is 135% of the foretriangle.
  • Spinnaker / cruising chute / gennaker Large light downwind sails. Fun, occasionally chaotic.
  • Head, tack, clew The three corners: top, front-bottom, back-bottom.
  • Luff, leech, foot The three edges: front, back, bottom.
  • Battens Stiffeners in pockets along the mainsail leech.
  • Roach The curved extra area of the leech beyond a straight line from head to clew.
  • Telltales Ribbons on the sail that show airflow.
  • Reef To reduce sail area. Slab reefing pulls the main down to reef points; furling rolls a sail around a foil or inside the mast or boom.
  • Sail cloth Woven polyester (Dacron) on almost all cruising boats of this class. Laminates are lighter and hold shape but die faster in the sun.

Running rigging: the ropes

  • Halyard Hoists a sail. Main halyard, genoa halyard, spinnaker halyard.
  • Sheet Controls a sail's angle to the wind. The mainsheet and two genoa sheets are the ropes you touch most.
  • Mainsheet The tackle from the boom to the traveller or the cockpit that controls the mainsail.
  • Outhaul Tensions the foot of the mainsail along the boom.
  • Cunningham Tensions the mainsail luff from the tack.
  • Reefing lines Pull the reef points down to the boom.
  • Furling line Rolls the headsail furler.
  • Guy Controls a spinnaker pole.
  • Preventer A line from the boom end forward to stop an accidental gybe.
  • Warp A mooring or anchoring rope. Bow line, stern line, springs are the standard set of four.
  • Painter The rope on the dinghy.

Below decks

Three ways of arranging the same 10 metres. Bow to the right in every case. Compare where the cockpit sits and what that does to the cabins.

  • Saloon The main cabin with seating and a table. The settees usually double as sea berths.
  • Settee / sea berth The bench seats along each side of the saloon. With a lee cloth rigged, the safest place to sleep at sea.
  • Saloon table Fixed or folding, often on the centreline or against one settee.
  • Galley The kitchen. The cooker swings on gimbals so it stays level as the boat heels. Almost always beside the companionway so the cook gets air.
  • Heads The toilet compartment, and the toilet itself.
  • Forecabin (forepeak) The bow cabin, usually with a V-shaped double berth. Lively at sea, lovely in harbour.
  • V-berth The two berths in the forecabin that meet at the bow, often with an infill to make a double.
  • Aft cabin The stern cabin. On a centre-cockpit boat it is a separate room with its own hatch; on an aft-cockpit boat it is tucked under the cockpit.
  • Quarter berth A single berth running aft under the cockpit side. Good sea berth, awkward to get into.
  • Chart table / nav station Where the charts, instruments and electrical switch panel live, usually beside the companionway.
  • Companionway steps The ladder down from the cockpit. On most boats the engine sits right behind it.
  • Engine bay Under the cockpit sole or the companionway steps on an aft-cockpit boat; under the cockpit on a centre-cockpit boat; under the wheelhouse on a motorsailer.
  • Wheelhouse The enclosed deckhouse of a motorsailer, with an inside steering position and often a dinette.
  • Hanging locker The wardrobe. Often opposite the heads.
  • Chain locker The compartment right in the bow where the anchor chain falls.
  • Bilge The lowest inside part of the hull, where water collects. The bilge pump empties it.
  • Sole The cabin floor. Deckhead the ceiling. Bulkhead an internal wall.
  • Lockers Cupboards. Stowage anywhere you put things.
  • Holding tank Stores toilet waste for pumping out ashore. Required in many of the seas covered here.
