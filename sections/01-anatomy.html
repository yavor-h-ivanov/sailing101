Anatomy and terminology

Sailing has its own vocabulary because precision matters when someone shouts an instruction across a windy boat. Learn these words first; everything else on this page uses them. Every label on the diagrams links to its definition, and most terms link back to a diagram with one click.

Seven words before anything else

Sloop
A boat with one mast and two sails: a mainsail behind the mast and a headsail in front of it. Every boat on this page is a sloop.
Line
A rope with a job. Almost no rope on a boat is called a rope.
Sheet
The line that pulls a sail in or lets it out. It is a rope, not a sail.
Halyard
The line that hoists a sail up the mast.
Helm
The wheel or tiller you steer with, and the person steering.
Cockpit
The open, sunken area, usually near the back of the boat, where you sit and steer.
Berth
A bed on a boat. Also a parking space in a harbour, which is why parking the boat is called berthing.

The boat in profile

A typical sloop of this size seen from the side, bow to the right, sails hoisted. This is the view you will see in every brochure and the one most terms are easiest to learn from. It is a masthead sloop: the forestay (the wire from the bow to the mast) goes to the very top of the mast. Drawn to proportion for a 10 m boat: the mast stands about 11 m above the deck. Directions and positions

  • Bow The front end. Forward is towards it.
  • Stern The back end. Aft is towards it; astern is behind the boat.
  • Port The left side when you face the bow. Its light at night is red.
  • Starboard The right side when you face the bow. Green light.
  • Quarter The back corners: the port quarter and the starboard quarter. Something "on the quarter" is behind and to one side.
  • Amidships The middle of the boat, lengthwise. "Midships!" as a steering order means centre the wheel.
  • Tack and gybe (the turns) Turning the boat so that the wind crosses from one side to the other. Turning the bow through the wind is a tack; turning the stern through it, with the wind behind you, is a gybe. Both make the boom swing across. See Sailing fundamentals.
  • Centreline The imaginary line from bow to stern down the middle of the boat. The mast and the keel sit on it.
  • Athwartships / fore-and-aft Across the boat / along the boat.
  • Inboard / outboard Towards the centreline / towards or beyond the side of the boat.
  • Windward The side the wind is coming from. Also called the weather side.
  • Leeward (say "loo-ard") The side away from the wind.
  • Ahead / astern In front of / behind the boat. Also the two gear directions of the engine.
  • Abeam At right angles to the centreline, out to the side.
  • Below Inside the cabin. On deck is outside.
  • Heel The boat leaning over under the push of the wind. Normal; heavy heel is slow.
  • Helm The steering position, the wheel or tiller itself, and the person steering ("who has the helm?").

Hull and underwater parts

  • Hull The watertight body of the boat. On this class, glass-reinforced plastic (GRP, "fibreglass").
  • Topsides The hull above the waterline.
  • Waterline Where the hull meets the water at rest.
  • Boot top The painted stripe just above the waterline that hides the dirty mark the water leaves.
  • Antifouling Paint below the waterline that slows weed and barnacle growth. Renewed every year or two.
  • Keel The fixed fin under the hull. Its heavy weight (the ballast) pulls the boat upright again after it heels, and its area stops the boat sliding sideways. Fin, bilge (twin) or long keel: see Hull, keel and rudder.
  • Ballast The weight, usually cast iron or lead, built into or bolted under the keel to keep the boat upright. Roughly 30–45% of the boat's weight on this class.
  • Rudder The steerable blade at the stern. Turned by a tiller or a wheel through the rudder stock, the shaft it hangs on.
  • Skeg A fixed fin ahead of the rudder that supports it. A spade rudder has no skeg and stands on its stock alone.
  • Propeller and shaft The propeller sits under the hull, aft of the keel, on a shaft from the engine. Some boats use a saildrive instead: a gearbox leg that hangs straight down through the hull, like the lower half of an outboard motor.
  • Transom The flat or near-flat surface across the stern. The bathing platform and boarding ladder are on or below it.
  • Stem The leading edge of the bow, where the two sides of the hull meet.
  • Sheer The curve of the deck edge seen from the side, usually lowest a little behind the middle.
  • Freeboard Height of the deck above the water. About a metre on this class.
  • Draught (draft) Depth from the waterline to the bottom of the keel. Decides where you can float: 1.1 m for a Finnsailer 35, up to 1.7 m for a deep-fin Sadler 32.
  • Length overall, waterline length, beam LOA is the hull from stem to stern (9.5–10.7 m for the boats on this page); LWL is the length at the waterline (the longer it is, the faster the boat can go); beam is the greatest width (3.1–3.5 m).
  • Displacement The boat's weight, equal to the water it pushes aside. About 3.7 to 6.2 tonnes for the five boats on this page.
  • Through-hull / skin fitting Any hole through the hull with a fitting: engine cooling water inlet, sink drains, the speed and depth sensors.
  • Seacock The valve on a through-hull that lets you shut it. Learn where every one is.
  • Stern gland The seal where the propeller shaft leaves the hull. It is allowed to drip a little; more than that sinks boats slowly.

The deck from above

The same boat with the sails down and the boom stowed on the centreline. This is where the working gear lives, and where you will spend most of your time when berthing (parking the boat in a harbour). Numbers run roughly from the bow aft and match the list beneath the drawing; both are clickable.

Deck and cockpit

  • Coachroof The raised cabin top, with the coachroof windows in its sides. Also called the cabin top.
  • Side deck The walkway between the coachroof and the edge of the deck. Narrow side decks make going forward awkward and wet.
  • Foredeck The deck forward of the mast, where the anchor and the headsail are handled.
  • Cockpit The sunken, open area near the back where the crew sits and steers.
  • Centre cockpit / aft cockpit Where the cockpit sits. A centre cockpit (Moody 33) leaves room for a separate aft cabin behind it; an aft cockpit (Sadler 32, Bavaria 1060) is more common and gives a larger saloon.
  • Coaming The raised edge around the cockpit that keeps water out and gives you a backrest. The primary winches usually sit on it.
  • Companionway The opening and steps from the cockpit down into the cabin, closed by a sliding hatch on top and washboards in front.
  • Sliding hatch The lid over the companionway that slides forward to open.
  • Washboards Removable boards that close the companionway opening in bad weather.
  • Sprayhood The folding canvas hood over the companionway. Keeps spray out of the cabin and off the helm. Americans call it a dodger; in Britain dodgers are the cloth panels on the guardrails.
  • Bimini A sun awning over the cockpit. Common on Mediterranean boats.
  • Pulpit The stainless-steel rail around the bow.
  • Pushpit The stainless-steel rail around the stern. Also called the stern rail or, in American usage, the stern pulpit.
  • Guardrails and stanchions The two wires along each side of the deck (also called lifelines) and the vertical posts that hold them up. About 60 cm high; the lower wire is there to stop a foot going through.
  • Jackstays Flat webbing straps running along each side deck from cockpit to bow. In rough weather you clip your safety harness to them before leaving the cockpit.
  • Toe rail The raised strip along the deck edge, often slotted aluminium, that stops your feet sliding off and gives you somewhere to attach blocks (pulleys).
  • Cleat A fitting for tying a rope to. There are usually two at the bow, two amidships (near the mast, for the springs, the diagonal mooring ropes) and two at the stern.
  • Fender The inflated rubber bumper you hang over the side to keep the hull off the pontoon or the boat next door.
  • Fairlead A fitting that guides a rope over the deck edge without rubbing it through, but does not hold it.
  • Winch A drum you wind a rope around so you can pull far harder than by hand. Self-tailing winches grip the rope for you. The largest, on the coamings, are the primaries for the genoa sheets.
  • Halyard winches Smaller winches on the coachroof (or on the mast on older boats) for hoisting sails and for reefing (making them smaller).
  • Clutch (jammer) A lever that grips a rope so you can take it off the winch and use the winch for another rope.
  • Mainsheet traveller A slider on a track across the boat. Moving it changes where the mainsheet pulls from, so you can move the boom sideways without pulling it down.
  • Genoa track and car A track along each side deck with a sliding fitting (the car) that holds the pulley the genoa sheet runs through, so you can change the angle of pull.
  • Chainplates The metal straps bolted to the hull or deck that the shrouds (the wires holding the mast up sideways) attach to, usually three a side for the cap shroud and the two lowers. A classic leak and corrosion point.
  • Hatch Any opening in the deck with a lid.
  • Forehatch The hatch on the foredeck over the forecabin. It doubles as the emergency exit.
  • Dorade vent A hooded air scoop on deck with a box under it, so air gets in but water drains back out. Named after the 1930s yacht that first carried them.
  • Cockpit locker Deep stowage under the cockpit seats for fenders, mooring ropes and everything else.
  • Cockpit drains Pipes from the cockpit floor to the sea, so a wave that fills the cockpit empties itself.
  • Gas locker A sealed locker for the cooking-gas bottles with a drain overboard, so leaking gas cannot collect in the bottom of the boat. Often built into the lazarette area, but never simply "in" it.
  • Lazarette The stern locker, right aft, often holding the steering gear and the emergency tiller.
  • Anchor locker The bow locker where the anchor chain is stowed.
  • Windlass The winch, manual or electric, that hauls the anchor chain in over the bow roller. Many older boats have none, and the chain comes up by hand.
  • Bow roller The fitting at the stem over which the anchor chain runs, and where the anchor usually lives.
  • Wheel or tiller What you steer with. A wheel stands on a pedestal in the cockpit; a tiller is a lever fixed straight onto the rudder stock. Every wheel-steered boat should also carry an emergency tiller for when the wheel gear fails; check that it is aboard.
  • Bathing platform / boarding ladder Stern access to the water. Usual on boats built since the 1990s, rare on the older British boats, which make do with a ladder hooked over the side.
  • Grab rails The wooden or steel handrails along the coachroof and below decks. "One hand for the boat" means one hand on one of these.
  • Bridgedeck The raised step between the cockpit and the companionway that stops a cockpit full of water pouring below. The mainsheet traveller often sits on it.

The rig seen from ahead

Looking at the boat from directly in front shows what the profile hides: the mast is held up sideways by wires called shrouds, which are pushed outwards by the spreaders and anchored to the hull at the chainplates. Lose one of those wires and the mast may well come down.

Rig: spars and standing rigging

The rig is everything that holds the sails up. Standing rigging is the fixed wire that keeps the mast up. Running rigging is the rope you adjust while sailing.

  • Mast The vertical pole (a spar) that holds the sails up. Anodised aluminium on this class. Deck-stepped masts sit on the coachroof; keel-stepped masts pass through the deck to the keel.
  • Masthead The top of the mast. It carries the pulleys (sheaves) the halyards run over, the wind instrument, the VHF radio aerial and usually an all-round white anchor light. Some boats also carry a tricolour navigation light up here; older boats have only deck-level navigation lights.
  • Boom The horizontal spar along the bottom of the mainsail. It swings across the boat when you tack or gybe (turn so the wind crosses to the other side). It hurts.
  • Gooseneck The hinge that joins the boom to the mast and lets it swing sideways and lift.
  • Masthead / fractional rig Where the forestay meets the mast: at the very top (masthead rig, most of this class) or part way up (fractional rig).
  • Forestay Wire from the masthead to the bow. Stops the mast falling backwards and carries the headsail, usually inside a furler.
  • Furler A drum at the bottom of the forestay and a hollow tube (the foil) around the wire: pull the furling line and the genoa rolls up around the tube like a blind.
  • Backstay Wire from the masthead to the stern. Stops the mast falling forwards. A split backstay forks to both quarters; an adjuster lets you tension it.
  • Shrouds The wires that hold the mast up sideways. Without them the mast would fall over the side.
  • Cap shrouds The shrouds that run from the masthead over the spreader tips down to the chainplates.
  • Lower shrouds Shorter shrouds from the base of the spreaders to the deck, one forward and one aft on each side, that stop the middle of the mast bending. From the side they make a narrow V.
  • Spreaders Horizontal struts part way up the mast that push the cap shrouds out, so the wires pull at a better angle.
  • Rigging screws (turnbuckles) Threaded fittings at the bottom of each wire for tensioning it. Locked with split pins or wire.
  • Spinnaker pole / whisker pole A spar that holds the outer corner of a downwind sail away from the boat, on the side opposite the mainsail. Its own lines, the uphaul and downhaul, hold it up and down.
  • Kicker (vang) A strut or rope-and-pulley system (a tackle) from the foot of the mast to the boom that stops the boom lifting. A rod kicker also holds the boom up when the sail is down.
  • Topping lift A line from the masthead to the end of the boom that holds the boom up when the sail is down.
  • Lazyjacks and stackpack Lines from the mast to the boom that catch the mainsail as it comes down, often with a zipped sail cover (the stackpack) fixed to the boom.

Sails and their parts

  • Mainsail The sail behind the mast, attached to the mast and the boom.
  • Headsail Any sail forward of the mast. A genoa is large and overlaps the mast; a jib is smaller and does not; a storm jib is tiny and heavy.
  • Genoa A large headsail whose back corner reaches aft of the mast. Sized by overlap: a "135% genoa" reaches back a distance equal to 135% of the mast-to-forestay distance.
  • Spinnaker / cruising chute / gennaker Large, light, colourful sails for sailing downwind. Fun, occasionally chaotic.
  • Head, tack, clew The three corners of a sail: top, front-bottom, back-bottom. ("Tack" the corner is a different word from "tack" the turn, and from "on port tack", which says which side the wind is on.)
  • How sails attach The mainsail's front edge runs up a groove in the mast on slides or a rope edge; a furling genoa's front edge runs up the groove in the furler tube; an old-style jib clips to the forestay with hanks.
  • Luff, leech, foot The three edges: front, back, bottom.
  • Battens Flat stiffeners in pockets along the back edge of the mainsail.
  • Roach The curved extra area of the mainsail's back edge beyond a straight line from head to clew.
  • Telltales Ribbons on the sail that show which way the air is flowing over it.
  • Reef To make a sail smaller in strong wind. Slab reefing lowers the mainsail part way and ties the loose fold to the boom; furling rolls a sail around the furler tube on the forestay, or inside the mast or boom.
  • Sail cloth Woven polyester (Dacron) on almost all cruising boats of this class. Laminates are lighter and hold shape but die faster in the sun.
  • Sail cover Canvas over the furled mainsail on the boom. Sunlight is what kills sails; the cover greatly extends their life.

Running rigging: the ropes

  • Line On a boat, ropes with a job are called lines. Each has its own name below.
  • Halyard A line that hoists a sail up the mast: main halyard, genoa halyard, spinnaker halyard.
  • Sheet A line that controls a sail's angle to the wind. Not the sail itself. The mainsheet and the two genoa sheets are the lines you touch most.
  • Mainsheet The rope-and-pulley system from the boom down to the traveller that pulls the mainsail in and lets it out.
  • Outhaul Tensions the bottom edge of the mainsail along the boom.
  • Cunningham Tensions the front edge of the mainsail from just above the tack.
  • Reefing lines Lines that pull the reinforced eyes (cringles) in the sail down to the boom when you reef. The small ties that gather the loose fold are the reef points.
  • Furling line Rolls the headsail up on its furler.
  • Guy The windward spinnaker sheet, which runs through the end of the pole and sets the pole's angle.
  • Working sheet / lazy sheet The genoa has two sheets, one each side. The one under load is the working sheet; the slack one is the lazy sheet. They swap every tack.
  • Shackle and block A shackle is a U-shaped metal link with a pin, for joining things. A block is a pulley.
  • Preventer A line from the end of the boom forward to the bow, to stop the boom slamming across in an accidental gybe when sailing downwind.
  • Warp A mooring or anchoring rope. Bow line, stern line and two springs (diagonal lines that stop the boat surging forwards and backwards) are the standard set of four.
  • Painter The rope on the front of the dinghy, the small rubber or rigid boat you row or motor ashore in.

Below decks

Four ways of arranging the same 10 metres, bow to the right in every case. Compare where the cockpit sits and what that does to the cabins. These are typical layouts; individual boats and years differ.

Where the cockpit goes decides everything else. An aft cockpit gives the largest saloon. A centre cockpit gives a separate aft cabin at the cost of a smaller saloon and a helm further from the stern when berthing. A motorsailer trades sailing performance for a heated wheelhouse with an inside helm. From around 1980, first on French production boats, builders learned to fit an aft double cabin under one side of an aft cockpit, which is why a Bavaria 1060 sleeps more people than a Sadler 32 a metre shorter.

  • Saloon The main cabin with seating and a table. The settees usually double as sea berths.
  • Settee / sea berth The bench seats along each side of the saloon. With a lee cloth (a canvas side that stops you rolling out) rigged, the safest place to sleep at sea.
  • Saloon table Fixed or folding, on the centreline or against one settee.
  • Galley The kitchen. The cooker swings on gimbals so it stays level as the boat heels. Almost always beside the companionway so the cook gets air.
  • Heads The toilet compartment, and the toilet itself. The word comes from the days when the crew's toilet was at the head (the bow) of the ship.
  • Forecabin The bow cabin, usually with a V-shaped double berth. Lively at sea, lovely in harbour.
  • Forepeak The very front compartment, in front of the forecabin's forward wall, where the anchor chain lives. Sailors also use the word loosely for the forecabin.
  • V-berth The two berths in the forecabin that meet at the bow, often with an infill cushion to make a double.
  • Aft cabin The stern cabin. On a centre-cockpit boat it is a separate room with its own hatch; on aft-cockpit boats from around 1980 onwards, and on most built after 1985, it is tucked under one side of the cockpit.
  • Quarter berth A single berth running aft under the cockpit side, typical of 1970s–80s boats. Good sea berth, awkward to climb into.
  • Chart table (navigation station) Where the charts (sea maps), instruments and electrical switch panel live, usually beside the companionway.
  • Companionway steps The ladder down from the cockpit. On most aft-cockpit boats the engine sits right behind it.
  • Engine bay Under the cockpit floor or behind the companionway steps on an aft-cockpit boat; under the cockpit on a centre-cockpit boat; under the wheelhouse floor on a motorsailer.
  • Wheelhouse The enclosed, glazed deckhouse of a motorsailer (a boat built to motor as much as to sail), with an inside steering position and often a table with seats (a dinette).
  • Hanging locker The wardrobe. Often opposite the heads.
  • Chain locker The compartment right in the bow where the anchor chain falls.
  • Tanks and batteries Fresh water and diesel tanks sit low under the berths or the cabin floor; the batteries live in a ventilated box near the engine. Know where each is and how much it holds.
  • Bilge The lowest inside part of the hull, under the floor, where water collects. The bilge pump empties it; know where its handle is kept.
  • Sole The cabin floor. Deckhead the ceiling. Bulkhead an internal wall.
  • Lockers Cupboards. Stowage anywhere you put things.
  • Holding tank Stores toilet waste for pumping out ashore. Required in many of the seas covered here.
