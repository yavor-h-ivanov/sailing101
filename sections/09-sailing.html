Sailing fundamentals

A sail is a wing, not a bag. Once that idea lands, points of sail, trim and heel all make sense, and the two manoeuvres that scare beginners, tacking and gybing, become routine.

Points of sail

The five ideas

  1. You cannot sail straight into the wind. A cruising boat of this class points about 40–50° off the true wind at best. To go upwind you zig-zag: beating.
  2. Sails are wings. Upwind the sail works by lift, like an aircraft wing on its side; only on a run is it a bag being pushed. Trim so the telltales stream: ease until the luff just starts to shake, then sheet in a little.
  3. The keel stops you sliding sideways and its ballast pulls the boat upright as the sails push it over. Heel is normal. Heel beyond about 20° is slow and uncomfortable: reef.
  4. Apparent wind is what you feel. Boat speed adds to true wind upwind and subtracts downwind. That is why a run feels calm and hides how much wind there really is.
  5. Reef early. If you are wondering whether to reef, you should already have done it.

Tacking

Turning the bow through the wind so the wind moves from one side to the other. Sails flap briefly, the genoa is released on the old side and sheeted in on the new one, the boom crosses the cockpit gently. The call is "Ready about" and then "Lee-oh" (or simply "Tacking") as the helm goes over.

Gybing

Turning the stern through the wind. The boom swings across with force because the sail stays full the whole way. Controlled gybe: centre the mainsheet first, turn slowly, ease the mainsheet out on the new side. An uncontrolled gybe is how people get hurt and booms break. A preventer stops accidental ones on a long downwind leg.

Reefing

Reducing sail. Roll some genoa away on the furler, and take a slab reef in the main: ease the halyard, hook the reef cringle at the tack, tension the halyard, pull the reefing line at the clew, tidy the bunt. Practise it in calm weather until it takes under three minutes.

Heaving to and man overboard

Heaving to parks the boat: tack without releasing the genoa sheet, then lash the helm to leeward. The boat sits quietly, forereaching slowly. Use it to reef, eat, think, or wait out a squall. Man overboard drills are practised, not read; but the shape is: shout, throw floating things, point continuously, press the MOB button, and return under sail or engine on a reach-and-return or a quick-stop pattern.
