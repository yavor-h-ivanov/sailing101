Start here

A one-page cheat sheet for getting into sailing on the kind of boat most people actually start with: a 30–36 ft (9–11 m) glass-fibre cruising yacht built between about 1970 and 2005, with an inboard diesel, a masthead or fractional sloop rig, and space for four to six people.

Who this is for

  • You are thinking about buying, chartering or crewing on a boat like a Moody 33, Sadler 32, Bavaria 1060, Gib’Sea 31/33 or Finnsailer 35.
  • You want the vocabulary, a mental model of how each part works, and a realistic list of what goes wrong.
  • You want one place to come back to instead of fifty browser tabs.
If you read nothing else
  1. Man overboard: shout, throw, point, press the MOB button, call for help. The drill.
  2. Mayday: the red DSC button, then channel 16. What to say.
  3. Gas: turn it off at the cylinder after every use; if you smell it, no switches, no flames. The routine.
  4. Seacocks and the bilge: know where every one is; check the bilge before you leave and every few hours at sea. The routine.
  5. Brief the crew before you leave: lifejackets, where the safety kit is, the boom, the engine, the radio. The briefing.
  6. Plan the passage: the weather, the tides, the dangers, and somewhere to run to. The plan.

How to use this page

  • Scroll or jump. The contents list follows you. Every heading has a # link you can copy and share.
  • Search. Press / and type a word such as cutless, gybe or osmosis. The glossary lists every word defined on the site.
  • The drawings are clickable. Every labelled part is a link: hover or tap it to read its definition under the drawing, and follow “Full entry” to the full explanation.
  • Read the short version first. Bullets and drawings are the cheat sheet; the folded cards and the “Sources and confidence” blocks are there when you want depth.
  • Cards follow one pattern. For a part of the boat: how it works, positives, negatives, common faults, what to check. For a problem: what happens, the good news, the bad news, the signs, what to do.
  • Units are metric: depth and length in metres, speed in knots, distance in nautical miles, wind in knots and Beaufort force.
How much to trust each section

Each section carries a status. Skeleton: headings and intent only. Draft: full content, checked by three reviewers (an experienced skipper, a beginner and a designer) until each scores it at least 8.5 out of 10, but not yet linked to all its sources. Complete: reviewed, with its facts linked to sources. Figures that could not be checked are marked TBC rather than guessed; ¹ means only one source was found, and ² that sources disagree.

This page is for learning. It is not a substitute for training, a current chart, the pilot book, or the boat’s own manuals.

A suggested learning path

  1. Anatomy and terminology: learn the names, so that everything else makes sense.
  2. Sailing fundamentals: points of sail, trim, tacking, gybing, reefing, man overboard.
  3. Manoeuvres under engine: the part that actually frightens beginners, in a marina.
  4. Hull, rig, deck, engine, systems and electronics: what each part does and how it fails.
  5. Navigation and the seas you will sail.
  6. Licences, and buying and owning.

The reference fleet in one paragraph

The five boats named above span the whole class. The Moody 33 is a British centre-cockpit cruiser with an aft cabin, with a fin keel or, on some boats, bilge keels. The Sadler 32 is a stiff, sea-kindly British aft-cockpit boat, a roomier development of its designer’s Contessa 32. The Bavaria 1060 is a German production cruiser of the mid-1980s with a fin keel, a spade rudder and a saildrive, from the years before Bavaria became a volume builder. The Gib’Sea 31 and 33 are French production cruisers for family sailing and charter. The Finnsailer 35 is a Finnish motorsailer with a wheelhouse, a long keel and a 75 hp diesel, built from 1969 for Baltic conditions. Between them they cover fin, bilge and long keels; tiller and wheel; shaft and saildrive; and three very different ideas of what a cruising boat is for. The details and their sources are in the fleet section.
