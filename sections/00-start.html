Start here

A one-page cheat sheet for getting into sailing on the kind of boat most people actually start with: a 9 to 11 m glass-fibre cruising yacht built between 1969 and about 2005, with a diesel engine inside the hull, one mast carrying a mainsail and a headsail (a sloop), and space for four to six people to sleep. New words are explained as they come, and every one is in the glossary.

Who this is for

  • You are thinking about buying, chartering or crewing on a boat like a Moody 33, Sadler 32, Bavaria 1060, Gib’Sea 31/33 or Finnsailer 35.
  • You want the vocabulary, a mental model of how each part works, and a realistic list of what goes wrong.
  • You want one place to come back to instead of fifty browser tabs.
If you read nothing else
  1. Stay on board: lifejacket on whenever you are on deck; clipped on with a harness at night, in rough weather, or when alone on deck; one hand for yourself and one for the boat. Cold water can kill in minutes. Jackstays and harnesses.
  2. Man overboard: shout, throw the lifebuoy, point, press the MOB (man overboard) button on the plotter, and call for help. Stop the boat close by, with the engine in neutral whenever the person is near the propeller, and have a way to lift them out ready: getting them back aboard is the hard part. Nobody goes into the water after them. The drill.
  3. Mayday (grave and imminent danger): lift the cover of the red DSC (digital distress) button on the radio and hold the button until the set says the alert has gone; it sends your position only if the radio is connected to a GPS. Then give the Mayday by voice on channel 16, high power. Serious but not life-threatening: Pan-Pan. What to say.
  4. Gas: off at the solenoid (the electric gas valve) or the cylinder after every use, and at the cylinder whenever you leave the boat. If you smell it: turn it off at the cylinder by hand, touch no switches and no flames, do not start the engine, open the hatches, and pump the bilge with the hand pump only. The routine.
  5. Water coming in: know where every seacock (the valves on the pipes through the hull) and bilge pump is. If water is rising: everyone pumping; find where it comes from (taste it: salt or fresh); close the seacocks; check the propeller-shaft seal and the engine hoses; push a soft wooden bung into any broken fitting; and call the coastguard early. The routine.
  6. Brief the crew before you leave: lifejackets, where the safety kit is, the fire extinguishers and fire blanket, the gas, the boom, the engine and the radio. The briefing.
  7. Plan the passage: the weather, the tides, the dangers, and somewhere to run to; and tell someone ashore where you are going and when you expect to arrive. The plan.

How to use this page

  • Scroll or jump. The contents list follows you. Every heading has a # link you can copy and share.
  • Search. Use the search box at the top (or press / on a keyboard) and type a word such as cutless, gybe or osmosis. The glossary gathers the words defined in every section.
  • The drawings are clickable. Every labelled part is a link: hover or tap it to read its definition under the drawing, and follow “Full entry” to the full explanation.
  • Read the short version first. Bullets and drawings are the cheat sheet; the folded cards and the “Sources and confidence” blocks are there when you want depth.
  • Cards follow one pattern. For a part of the boat: how it works, positives, negatives, common faults, what to check. For a problem: what happens, the good news, the bad news, the signs, what to do.
  • Photos and videos. Photos are Creative Commons licensed or public domain, with the photographer and licence under each one. Videos are linked, not embedded: each was chosen by its title, channel and description, and is listed by section in the video library.
  • Units are metric: depth and length in metres, speed in knots, distance in nautical miles, wind in knots and Beaufort force.
How much to trust each section

Each section carries a status. Skeleton: headings and intent only. Draft: full content, checked in repeated AI review passes written from three points of view (an experienced skipper, a beginner and a designer) until each scores it at least 8.5 out of 10. These are not checks by people. Complete: reviewed the same way, with its facts linked to sources. Figures that could not be checked are marked TBC rather than guessed; ¹ means only one source was found, and ² that sources disagree.

This page is for learning. It is not a substitute for training, a current chart, the pilot book, or the boat’s own manuals.

A suggested learning path

  1. Anatomy and terminology: learn the names, so that everything else makes sense.
  2. Sailing fundamentals: points of sail, trim, tacking, gybing, reefing, man overboard.
  3. Manoeuvres under engine: the part that actually frightens beginners, in a marina.
  4. Hull, rig, deck, engine, systems and electronics: what each part does and how it fails.
  5. Navigation and the seas you will sail.
  6. Licences, and buying and owning.
  7. Glossary, videos and reading: come back whenever a word or a job is new.
  8. Get on a real boat. Book an RYA Competent Crew course (five days on a yacht with an instructor), or crew for an experienced skipper, before you buy; the page is no substitute.

The reference fleet in one paragraph

The five boats named above span the whole class. The Moody 33 is a British centre-cockpit cruiser with an aft cabin, with a fin keel or, on some boats, bilge keels. The Sadler 32 is a stiff (it resists heeling) and sea-kindly (it moves gently in waves) British aft-cockpit boat, a roomier development of its designer’s Contessa 32. The Bavaria 1060 is a German production cruiser of the mid-1980s with a fin keel, usually with a spade rudder and a saildrive, from before the high-volume ranges of the 1990s. The Gib’Sea 31 and 33 are French production cruisers for family sailing and charter. The Finnsailer 35 is a Finnish motorsailer (a boat built to motor as much as to sail) with a wheelhouse, a long keel and a diesel of about 75 hp, built from 1969 for Baltic conditions. Between them they cover fin, bilge and long keels; tiller and wheel; shaft and saildrive; and three very different ideas of what a cruising boat is for. The details and their sources are in the fleet section.
