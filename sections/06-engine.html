Engine and drivetrain

A small marine diesel of 18–50 hp turning a propeller through a gearbox and either a shaft or a saildrive. Simple, long-lived, and the single component most likely to spoil your day when neglected.

What you will find

  • Engines: Yanmar (GM and YM series), Volvo Penta (MD and D series), Bukh, Perkins and Perkins-based Volvo, Beta Marine (Kubota-based) as a common repower, Vetus (Mitsubishi-based), Nanni (Kubota-based). Older British boats may still carry a raw-water-cooled Volvo MD or a petrol Vire TBC.
  • Cooling: indirect (fresh water in the engine, sea water through a heat exchanger) on almost everything after 1980; direct raw-water cooling on older engines.
  • Drive: shaft with a stern gland and P-bracket or skeg bearing, or a saildrive leg through the hull (Bavaria 1060 typically).

Stern gland (shaft seal)

also: stuffing box, packed gland, dripless seal, lip seal

Where the propeller shaft leaves the hull, something has to let it spin while keeping the sea out. On this class you will meet two families: the traditional packed stuffing box and the "dripless" face or lip seal.

How it works
  • Stuffing box: rings of greased flax or PTFE packing are squeezed around the shaft by a threaded follower. It is meant to drip slowly while the shaft turns (a couple of drops a minute) and to stop, or nearly stop, at rest.
  • Face seal (PSS type): a carbon ring on a rubber bellows presses against a stainless collar fixed to the shaft. No drips. Needs a vent or water feed above the waterline so it does not run dry.
  • Lip seal (Volvo type): a rubber lip seal in a housing runs directly on the shaft. Needs "burping" of air after launch and periodic greasing.
Positives
  • Stuffing box: cheap, repackable afloat in an emergency, fails slowly and visibly.
  • Dripless: dry bilge, no adjustment, no wear on the shaft.
Negatives
  • Stuffing box: constant drip, wears a groove in the shaft over decades, needs adjusting.
  • Dripless: the bellows ages (replace every 6–10 years TBC), and a failure can be a sudden flood rather than a drip.
Common faults
  • Over-tightened stuffing box that gets hot and scores the shaft.
  • Hose clips rusted through on the flexible hose joining gland to stern tube.
  • Face seal collar slipped along the shaft, letting the carbon face open.
  • Lip seal run dry after launch and now leaking.
What to check
  • Feel the gland after a motoring passage: warm is fine, hot is not.
  • Count the drips at rest and under way.
  • Two hose clips on every hose, both stainless, neither rusted.
  • Ask when the bellows or lip seal was last replaced. No answer means now.
More detail: shaft versus saildrive

A shaft drive puts the gearbox output through a flexible coupling, a shaft, the stern gland, a cutless (rubber) bearing in a P-bracket or skeg, and the propeller. It is robust and every yard can work on it, but alignment matters and the shaft angle means the propeller pushes slightly downward.

A saildrive is an outboard-style leg bolted through a hole in the hull, sealed by a large rubber diaphragm. The propeller is horizontal and further forward, giving better manoeuvring and less vibration, and the engine sits lower. The diaphragm has a manufacturer replacement interval (commonly seven years TBC), the leg is aluminium and vulnerable to galvanic corrosion, and antifouling must be copper-free.
