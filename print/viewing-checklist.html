Viewing a used yacht: the checklist

Compiled from the buyer’s checklists in each section of Sailing 101; the reasons behind every item are there. Tick as you go, note what you find, and take photographs. A checklist does not replace a surveyor.

First look round the boat

  • Hull below the waterline (out of the water, or ask for a lift): blisters (see osmosis), old repairs, a wavy surface where the hull has been filled and faired. Moisture readings are for the surveyor.
  • Keel joint: a thin crack along the front of the joint between keel and hull (the “smile”) can be only filler, or the sign of a grounding. Inside, look at the keel bolts and the floors (the frames they pass through) for rust, weeping and cracks.
  • Rudder: grip the bottom and shake it: any clunk is wear in the bearings. Water weeping from the blade after lifting out means water inside.
  • Stern gland or saildrive: drips, corrosion, and the age of the saildrive’s rubber seal, which Volvo Penta asks to be replaced every seven years (many owners go longer ).
  • Seacocks and hoses: every one should turn; look for green or pink corrosion on bronze and brass, and two clips on every hose below the waterline.
  • Chainplates: rust stains, leaks and soft deck where they pass through; inside, the bulkheads or knees they are bolted to.
  • Deck: walk every part of it. A soft or springy area means the core under the skin is wet; tap it with a coin or a screwdriver handle: a dull sound instead of a sharp one means trouble. Crazing round fittings and stanchions can mean leaks.
  • Mast step: on a deck-stepped mast, a sagging deck around it, or a crushed or rotten post below, are serious.
  • Standing rigging: ask its age. Look for cracks in the swaged terminals, broken strands and rust. Many surveyors and insurers ask for rigging older than about ten years to be replaced, though insurers vary.
  • Sails: unroll them. Look for cloth that feels papery or chalky with sun damage, stretched and baggy shape, failed stitching and the UV strip on a furling genoa; ask before handling a seller’s sails roughly.
  • Engine: ask for it to be cold when you arrive, and start it yourself. It should start in seconds and settle; watch the exhaust for smoke and water (see engine faults), look for oil in the bilge, cracked mounts, and ask for the service history.
  • Below: stains on the headlining and lockers show old leaks; smell for damp, diesel and sewage. Look at the wiring behind the switch panel, the batteries’ dates, and the gas installation (see Boat systems).

Hull, keel and rudder

  • Walk round twice. First for the shape: sight along the topsides for flats and bumps from repairs, and along the keel joint from ahead and astern. Then for the surface: blisters, crazing, repairs, rust runs.
  • Keel. Joint hairline or smile? Rust at the joint? Inside, over the keel: cracked filler, wet nuts, cracked or lifted grid.
  • Rudder. Rock it for play, turn it for stiffness, look for drips from the bottom edge and cracks at the skeg root.
  • Every hole. Count the skin fittings outside, then find each seacock inside. Scrape, turn, look at the hose and the clips.
  • Deck. Bare feet and a plastic hammer around every fitting; ceiling and lockers for stains.
  • Ask three questions. Has she been aground? When were the keel bolts last looked at? When was she last metered for moisture, and what did it read against the topsides?
  • Then pay a surveyor. Out of the water, chosen by you. This list is for deciding which boats are worth the survey fee.

Rig and sails

  • Age first. Ask when the standing rigging was replaced and who did it. No answer means original. Ten years is the working life; a survey will say so.
  • Every lower terminal. Magnifier, straight edge, gloved hand up the wire. Cracks down the swage, proud strands, rust weeping from the top, bent forks.
  • Every rigging screw. Split pins present, opened and taped; toggles at the bottom (both ends on the forestay); nothing leaning or bearing on one side; threads not seized or run out.
  • Chainplates. Rust at the deck plates, soft deck inboard of the shrouds, and the bulkhead behind them from inside the lockers.
  • Mast foot. Deck-stepped: a dip in the coachroof and the compression post below. Keel-stepped: the partners for leaks and, when the mast is out, the heel for corrosion.
  • Furler. Look up at the halyard angle at the swivel; turn the drum by hand; count the foil screws; ask when the forestay inside was last seen.
  • Boom. Gooseneck play, kicker bracket cracks, reefing lines inside the boom, sheaves at the end.
  • Sails. Hoist them. Stitching, UV strip, batten pockets, cringles, and shape: a leech that hooks and a bag that will not flatten mean new sails within the price.
  • Then aloft, or a rigger aloft: masthead fittings, T-terminals, spreader roots and tips, the backstay’s top end, every split pin. Photographs of everything, dated.

Deck hardware and steering

  • Steer it. Wheel from stop to stop with the boat still: play, notches, stiffness. Then open the locker and watch the quadrant while someone turns the wheel.
  • Find the emergency tiller and fit it. On a hydraulic boat, find the bypass valve.
  • Every wire, chain and sheave on a cable system: rag test, chain centred, stops meeting their supports. Ask how old the wire is.
  • Anchor gear. Read the anchor type and weight; check the chain against the gypsy stamp and count how much there is; look at the windlass terminals and the breaker.
  • Winches. Spin each drum: it should click one way and lock the other. Ask when they were last serviced.
  • Stanchions and cleats. Push and pull every one; look underneath for backing plates and stains.
  • Windows and hatches. Crazing, gaskets, stains on the lining below; hose the boat and sit inside.
  • Drains and lockers. Bucket down the cockpit drains and the gas-locker drain; seals on every locker lid.
  • Then the surveyor, who will moisture-meter the deck around every fitting you have just looked at.

Engine and drivetrain

  • Cold start. Ask for the engine not to be run before you arrive; feel it is cold, then watch it start. Smoke that clears in a minute is normal.
  • Water at the exhaust within a few seconds of starting, and no alarm lights.
  • Dipstick and coolant. Oil black is normal in a diesel, but not milky or grey, and not smelling of diesel; coolant clean, not rusty, at the right level.
  • Fuel. Look in the primary filter bowl for water and black slime; ask when the tank was cleaned.
  • Underneath. Torch under the engine: oil, diesel, coolant or rust in the drip tray and bilge; the state of the mounts.
  • The sea-water side. The strainer, the impeller’s age, the exhaust elbow for weeping rust, the waterlock and the anti-siphon valve.
  • Drive. Stern gland and its hose clips, or the saildrive diaphragm date on an invoice; cutless bearing and propeller on the hard.
  • Under way. Full revs in ahead for a few minutes: it should reach the rated revs without black smoke or overheating. Astern firmly. No vibration at any speed.
  • Paperwork. Engine hours, service invoices, a manual on board; for an old engine, the price of an impeller, a filter set and a gasket kit.
  • Then the engineer. An oil analysis and a compression test tell you more about a forty-year-old engine than anything you can see.

Boat systems

  • Batteries: dates, terminals, straps; resting voltages; which switch does what.
  • Behind the panel: fuses on every circuit, labelled cables, no household wire or taped joints.
  • Charging: with the engine running, about 14 V or more at each bank; the charger and solar settings match the batteries.
  • Shore power: press the RCD test button; look at the shore lead; ask about the anodes.
  • Water: run the pump with everything shut and listen; run each tap hot and cold after the engine has run.
  • Gas: the bucket test on the locker drain; the hose date; a bubble test; the flame-failure devices on every burner.
  • Heater: run it for twenty minutes; follow the exhaust pipe from end to end; a CO alarm in date.
  • Heads: both seacocks and both vented loops found; flush and dry; the rag test on the hoses; where the holding tank is and how it empties.
  • Bilge: lift the float and hear the pump; work the manual pump; a bung by every seacock.

Electronics

  • Depth: it reads, and you know whether below the keel or the surface.
  • VHF: a radio check with the marina; DSC, the MMSI and a GPS position on the set’s screen.
  • AIS: the boat appears on a phone app; targets appear on the plotter.
  • Plotter or tablet: a position fix, and charts no more than a year or two old.
  • Log and wind: readings change as the boat moves; the mast-foot connector is clean.
  • Compass: no bubble; the card swings freely; the light works.
  • Lights: every navigation light, one switch at a time, at dusk.
  • Beacons: EPIRB and PLB battery dates and registrations.
  • Behind the panel: a drawing of the network, or the start of one.
